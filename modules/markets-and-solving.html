Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Imagine an auction hall that never sleeps. In every five-year period GCAM calls out prices for oil, electricity, crops, land and water. Producers say how much they would supply at those prices, consumers say how much they would buy, and the auctioneer raises prices where buyers want more than sellers offer, and lowers them where there is too much. When every market balances at the same time, the period is solved and the model moves on. That balance is called equilibrium, and the gap between demand and supply is called excess demand. In this module you first clear one market by hand, finding the price where demand equals supply. Then you solve three linked markets together, because a change in the oil price shifts demand for electricity and biomass too. GCAM does this for a few hundred markets at once, using Broyden's method, a clever cousin of Newton's method that works in log prices. You will also see why GCAM is recursive-dynamic: it solves one period at a time, without knowing the future.

What this component represents

"GCAM operates by determining a set of prices that ensure supply is equal to demand for all time steps" (marketplace.html). Each market is a good in a place: energy carriers in EJ per year, crops and land, and water by basin. Supply and demand sectors are representative agents that submit bids; the solver adjusts prices until all markets clear.

This is partial equilibrium: the energy, land and water markets are solved in detail, while the wider economy enters through GDP.

One market by hand

Take the toy electricity market from week 4: demand 1000 − 4p and supply 6(p − 0.9·tax). Excess demand is

F(p) = (1000 − 4p) − 6(p − 0.9·tax)

With no tax, set F = 0: 1000 − 4p = 6p, so 10p = 1000 and p = 100, quantity 600. With a $50 tax: 1000 − 4p = 6p − 270, so 10p = 1270 and p = 127, quantity 492. A root finder (bisection, Brent) finds the same answer by searching for the sign change of F.

A useful mental model

Picture a thermostat for each market. Too much demand: the price heats up. Too much supply: it cools down. The solver turns all the thermostats at once, knowing that warming one room changes the others.

Many linked markets at once

Real markets are linked: a carbon price raises the cost of burning oil, which shifts demand toward electricity and biomass, which changes their prices, which feeds back on oil. So all prices must be found together. The kit's three-market toy (oil, electricity, biomass) needs a vector root finder. With a $50 carbon price it settles at oil 9.04, electricity 31.60 and biomass 5.25, with every excess demand below 2×10−10.

find p such that Fm(p1, …, pM) = 0 for every market m

How GCAM's solver works

GCAM works in log prices with excess demand scaled to a common size. Its main method is Broyden's method: it solves B·δx = −F(x), where B is a cheap secant approximation of the Jacobian (how every market's gap reacts to every price), and shortens the step if a step makes ‖F‖ worse. Bisection moves prices out of flat or singular regions, and a preconditioner fixes bad starting guesses (solver.html).

The Jacobian covers "usually a few hundred" solved markets. Example settings from the sample configuration: solution-tolerance 0.001, solution-floor 0.0001, max-model-calcs 2500 (the defaults themselves are not stated in the docs).

In GCAM

The solver is configured in input/solution/cal_solver_config.xml (pointed to by solver_config in configuration.xml). Check exe/logs/main_log.txt: a good run ends with "Model exiting successfully" and lists any unsolved markets. Query prices of all markets to see the solved prices (energy in 1975$/GJ) (user-guide.html, outputs_prices.html).

Recursive dynamics: no crystal ball

GCAM is dynamic recursive: agents decide with what they know in the current period, then the model steps forward. It is not an intertemporal optimisation, so it can build a coal plant in 2030 that a policy in 2040 makes regrettable (overview.html). Optimisation models such as MESSAGEix instead choose the whole century at once with perfect foresight (Go 2.8 compares the two).

"The model failed, so the result is still usable"

An unsolved period means some market did not clear: its prices and quantities are not an equilibrium. Never report results from a run with unsolved markets; fix tolerances, starting prices or the input that caused it.

# Module 2.2 - Markets, equilibrium and solving (toy numbers)
# Part A: one market (lab 4). Part B: three linked markets solved together.
excess <- function(p, tax) (1000 - 4 * p) - 6 * (p - 0.9 * tax)
for (tax in c(0, 50)) {
  p <- uniroot(excess, c(0, 500), tax = tax)$root
  cat(sprintf("One market, tax %2d: price %6.1f, quantity %6.1f\n", tax, p, 1000 - 4 * p))
}

excess3 <- function(p, carbon = 0) {
  oil <- p[1]; elec <- p[2]; bio <- p[3]; oc <- oil + 0.07 * carbon
  d <- c(100 * (oc / 10)^-0.6 * (elec / 30)^0.3, 80 * (elec / 30)^-0.5 * (oc / 10)^0.3, 20 * (bio / 5)^-0.8 * (oc / 10)^0.5)
  s <- c(100 * (oil / 10)^1.2, 80 * (elec / 30) * (bio / 5)^-0.2, 20 * (bio / 5)^1.5)
  d - s
}
# A tiny Newton solver in log-prices (GCAM uses Broyden's method, a cheaper cousin of Newton).
solve_markets <- function(carbon, p = c(8, 25, 6), iters = 50) {
  x <- log(p)
  f <- function(x) excess3(exp(x), carbon) / c(100, 80, 20)
  for (i in 1:iters) {
    fx <- f(x)
    if (max(abs(fx)) < 1e-10) break
    J <- sapply(1:3, function(j) { h <- 1e-6; xh <- x; xh[j] <- xh[j] + h; (f(xh) - fx) / h })
    x <- x - solve(J, fx)
  }
  list(price = exp(x), iterations = i, max_excess = max(abs(excess3(exp(x), carbon))))
}
for (cp in c(0, 50)) {
  s <- solve_markets(cp)
  cat(sprintf("Three markets, carbon %2d: oil %.2f, electricity %.2f, biomass %.2f (%d iterations, max excess %.1e)\n",
              cp, s$price[1], s$price[2], s$price[3], s$iterations, s$max_excess))
}
Base R: uniroot for one market, then a small Newton iteration in log prices. Converges in 5 iterations.

Remember

  • Equilibrium means supply equals demand in every market at the same time
  • Excess demand F(p) is the gap the solver drives to zero
  • Linked markets must be solved together; GCAM uses Broyden's method in log prices
  • A solved period needs every market within tolerance; check the log
  • GCAM solves one 5-year period at a time with no foresight

Check yourself

1. Demand 1000 − 4p, supply 6p. The equilibrium price is…

1000 − 4p = 6p gives p = 100.

2. GCAM's primary solution method is…

Broyden's quasi-Newton method, helped by bisection and a preconditioner (solver.html).

3. Why must linked markets be solved together?

Cross-effects (oil vs electricity vs biomass) mean each market's balance depends on the other prices.

4. "Recursive-dynamic" means GCAM…

No foresight: each period is solved and then fixed.

5. A run ends with unsolved markets in 2050. You should…

Unsolved markets are not an equilibrium; downstream periods are affected too.

Words and links

New words

Equilibrium
A set of prices at which supply equals demand in every market.
Excess demand
Demand minus supply at a given price; zero at equilibrium.
Broyden's method
A quasi-Newton root finder that updates an approximate Jacobian cheaply each step.
Jacobian
The table of how each market's excess demand changes when each price changes.
Recursive dynamics
Solving one period at a time using only current information.
Partial equilibrium
Detailed markets for some sectors, with the rest of the economy taken as given.
