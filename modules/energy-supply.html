Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Energy supply in GCAM starts in the ground and in the sky. Coal, oil, gas and uranium are depletable resources described by graded supply curves: the cheap grades are used first, and the price climbs as more is extracted. Wind and solar are flows rather than stocks, described by renewable supply curves. These primary resources feed conversion sectors such as power plants, refineries and hydrogen production. Each technology has a cost built from three parts: a non-energy cost that covers building and running the plant, a fuel cost, and an emissions cost under any carbon price. In this module you will compute a levelised cost of electricity for a gas plant and a solar farm, and see the gas cost rise by about eighteen dollars for every fifty dollars of carbon price. You will also meet the value factor. As wind and solar take a larger share of the grid, each extra unit is worth less, so GCAM divides their cost by a value factor that falls with market share. That simple rule keeps renewables from taking over unrealistically fast.

Resources: stocks and flows

Depletable resources (coal, oil, unconventional oil, gas, uranium) sit on graded supply curves: as the price rises, more of the resource becomes economic. Technical change can lower extraction costs over time (supply_energy.html). In the kit's toy curve, cost rises from $3/GJ to $12/GJ across four grades, so the price at 200, 800 and 1400 EJ of cumulative use is 3.6, 6.0 and 11.0 $/GJ.

Renewables are annual flows:

Q = Qmax · Pb / (Pmidb + Pb)

where Pmid is the price at which half the maximum is produced.

Technology cost

Every technology's cost per unit of output is

C = non-energy cost + Σ (input price × input coefficient) + Σ (GHG price × emissions) − secondary output value

For power plants, non-energy cost is levelised: overnight capital × capital recovery factor ÷ (capacity factor × 8.76) + fixed and variable O&M (en_technologies.html). The kit's gas plant (capex $1,000/kW, capacity factor 0.55, fuel $6/GJ, efficiency 55%) costs 61.2 $/MWh with no carbon price, 79.5 at $50/t and 97.9 at $100/t. The solar farm (capex $900/kW, CF 0.22) costs 45.4 $/MWh at any carbon price. Illustrative numbers.

In GCAM

Power plant costs come from NREL ATB files (NREL_ATB_capital.csv, NREL_ATB_OMfixed.csv, NREL_ATB_OMvar.csv); efficiencies, capture rates and retirement in A23.globaltech_*.csv (inputs_supply.html). Units: capital-overnight in 1975$/kW, prices in 1975$/GJ. Query elec gen by subsector to see the mix. Plant-specific values are not printed on the doc pages.

Wind and solar: the value factor

Since v7.3 GCAM no longer pairs wind and solar with backup turbines. Instead their levelised cost is divided by a value factor that falls linearly with their market share (CMP 394):

PLCOE = LCOE / VF, VF = intercept + slope × share

For PV the intercept is 0.972 and the slope −1.443 (then divided by 0.903, the non-VRE average); onshore wind 0.91 and −1.2. The relationships come from ReEDS for the contiguous US and may break down above about 75% VRE. Storage is not modelled explicitly.

A useful mental model

The first solar farm in a region sells power at midday when it is valuable. The hundredth floods the same hours and earns less. The value factor charges that crowding as a higher effective cost.

Electricity, fuels and hydrogen

Electricity is nested: fuel subsector → technology → cooling system (up to five types). Refining covers oil, biomass liquids (up to 8 technologies per region, some with CCS), coal-to-liquids and gas-to-liquids. Hydrogen has 10 central and 2 forecourt routes from up to 7 primary sources. CCS is a remove-fraction on emissions sent to a storage market; direct air capture enters with a share weight rising to 1 by 2050 (supply_energy.html, details_energy.html).

Vintages and retirement

Plants built in one period keep running: GCAM tracks vintages. Three shutdown rules exist: profit shutdown (output falls as variable cost approaches price), an S-curve lifetime, and exogenous output scalars, such as a 2015 US coal plant at 0.5 in 2021 falling to 0 by 2040 (en_technologies.html). This is why the fleet changes more slowly than new investment.

"GCAM learns: costs fall as capacity grows"

In the released model, future technology costs are inputs you choose; the model does not learn during a run. Learning curves are one way to write those inputs (the kit computes $900/kW falling to $460.8/kW after three doublings at 20%).

# Module 2.4 - Energy supply: levelised cost, resource supply curve, learning (toy numbers)
crf <- function(r, n) r * (1 + r)^n / ((1 + r)^n - 1)
lcoe <- function(capex, fom, cf, fuel_price = 0, eff = 1, ef = 0, carbon = 0, r = 0.07, n = 30) {
  mwh <- cf * 8.76
  (capex * crf(r, n) + fom) / mwh + fuel_price * 3.6 / eff + ef * 3.6 / eff * carbon
}
for (cp in c(0, 50, 100))
  cat(sprintf("carbon %3d: gas %5.1f, solar %5.1f $/MWh\n", cp,
      lcoe(1000, 25, 0.55, 6, 0.55, 0.0561, cp), lcoe(900, 15, 0.22)))
grades <- data.frame(q = c(0, 500, 1000, 1500), cost = c(3, 4.5, 7, 12))
resource_price <- function(cum) approx(grades$q, grades$cost, xout = cum, rule = 2)$y
cat("resource price at 200, 800, 1400 EJ:", resource_price(c(200, 800, 1400)), "\n")
cat("solar capex after 3 doublings at 20%:", 900 * (1 - 0.20)^3, "$/kW\n")

Remember

  • Fossil fuels come from graded supply curves; renewables from flow supply curves
  • Technology cost = non-energy cost + fuel cost + emissions cost
  • A carbon price raises each option's cost in proportion to its emissions
  • Wind and solar costs are divided by a value factor that falls with their share (v7.3+)
  • Future costs are inputs, not learned by the model; vintages make the fleet change slowly

Check yourself

1. In GCAM v8.2/9.1, the integration cost of wind and solar is represented by…

PLCOE = LCOE / VF since v7.3; storage is not explicit.

2. The kit gas plant costs 61.2 $/MWh at zero carbon price and 79.5 at $50/t. At $100/t it costs about…

Each $50/t adds about 18.3 $/MWh, so 61.2 + 36.7 ≈ 97.9.

3. A graded resource supply curve means…

Higher grades are dearer to extract.

4. Does GCAM compute technology learning during a run?

Costs are inputs; learning curves can be used to choose them.

Words and links

New words

Levelised cost (LCOE)
All lifetime costs of a plant divided by its lifetime output, in $/MWh.
Capacity factor
Actual output divided by the output if the plant ran at full power all year.
Value factor
The value of variable renewable output relative to average power; falls as their share grows.
Graded supply curve
A resource split into grades of rising extraction cost.
Vintage
The cohort of plants built in one period, which keeps operating for its lifetime.
