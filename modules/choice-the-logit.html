Spoken overview
Read the transcript

If coal costs sixty dollars, gas seventy and solar eighty, a simple model would build only coal. Real investors do not behave like that. Sites differ, preferences differ, costs vary from place to place, so cheaper options win more of the market but not all of it. GCAM captures this with the logit, a formula that turns costs into market shares. Each option's cost is raised to a negative power, called the logit exponent, and divided by the sum over all options. A small exponent spreads the market widely. A large exponent behaves almost like winner takes all. Share weights are extra numbers calibrated so that the base year reproduces the shares we actually observe; they carry everything the costs leave out. Choices are also nested: electricity first chooses between fuels, then between technologies within each fuel, then between cooling systems. In this module you will compute shares by hand, calibrate share weights, and build a two-level nest in code.

Why not cheapest-takes-all?

A model that always picks the single cheapest technology flips its whole mix when costs cross, which real markets never do. Costs vary across sites and investors; some preferences are unmeasured. The logit turns costs into smooth market shares: cheaper wins more, never everything (choice.html).

The relative-cost logit

si = αi · ciγ / Σj αj · cjγ, γ < 0

With equal share weights and γ = −4, coal $60, gas $70, solar $80: 60−4 = 7.72×10−8, 70−4 = 4.16×10−8, 80−4 = 2.44×10−8. The sum is 14.32×10−8, so the shares are 54%, 29% and 17%. With γ = −1 they are 38/33/29%; with γ = −10, 79/17/4%.

Try it

Coal $60 and gas $70, γ = −2, equal weights. Coal's share? 60−2 = 2.78×10−4, 70−2 = 2.04×10−4, so coal = 2.78 / 4.82 = 58%.

The absolute-cost logit

si = αi · exp(β · ci) / Σj αj · exp(β · cj)

Here the share ratio depends on the cost difference rather than the ratio. GCAM users always enter a logit exponent; for the absolute form GCAM derives β from it. The absolute form behaves well when costs can be near zero or negative, and GCAM uses it in places such as direct air capture and the passenger transport nests (choice.html).

Share weights and calibration

Share weights are set so that, at base-year costs, the logit reproduces the observed 2021 shares. If observed shares are 30/50/20% at costs 60/70/80 and γ = −4, then α ∝ share / cγ, giving weights 1 : 3.088 : 2.107 (normalised to coal). They also phase in new technologies: low in their first year, rising toward a neutral value. Future share weights are assumptions, so treat them as a scenario lever, not a fact.

In GCAM

Logit exponents are in gcamdata assumption files A21.subsector_logit.csv … A26.subsector_logit.csv; share-weight interpolation rules in A21.subsector_interp.csv and similar (inputs_supply.html). In XML they appear as <logit-exponent> and <share-weight> under each subsector and technology.

Nesting

Choice in GCAM is layered: sector → subsector → technology → (for thermal power) cooling system, each with its own logit (details_energy.html). The reference example uses γ = −3 between electricity subsectors and γ = −10 among coal technologies. In the kit's two-level nest (fossil nest with γ = −6 inside, then fossil vs solar with γ = −3) the result is coal 48.2%, gas 19.1%, solar 32.6%. Land and trade use logits too (Go 2.6).

"A share weight is a subsidy"

No. A share weight is a calibrated preference multiplier with no money attached. Changing future share weights changes results without changing any cost, so state those assumptions openly.

# Module 2.3 - Choice: the logit (relative-cost form as in GCAM's economic choice docs)
relative_logit <- function(costs, g, w = rep(1, length(costs))) { x <- w * costs^g; x / sum(x) }
absolute_logit <- function(costs, b, w = rep(1, length(costs))) { x <- w * exp(b * costs); x / sum(x) }
costs <- c(coal = 60, gas = 70, solar = 80)
for (g in c(-1, -4, -10)) { cat("exponent", g, ": "); print(round(relative_logit(costs, g), 3)) }
# Calibrate share weights so the base year reproduces observed shares
observed <- c(0.30, 0.50, 0.20); g <- -4
w <- observed / costs^g; w <- w / w[1]
cat("share weights:", round(w, 3), " -> shares:", round(relative_logit(costs, g, w), 3), "\n")
# Two-level nest: fossil (coal, gas) vs solar
fossil <- relative_logit(c(60, 70), -6); fossil_cost <- sum(c(60, 70) * fossil)
top <- relative_logit(c(fossil_cost, 80), -3)
cat(sprintf("nested: coal %.3f, gas %.3f, solar %.3f\n", top[1] * fossil[1], top[1] * fossil[2], top[2]))
Identical numbers in base R.

Remember

  • The logit turns costs into smooth shares: cheaper wins more, never everything
  • The exponent sets how sensitive shares are to cost differences
  • Share weights calibrate the base year and phase in new options; future values are assumptions
  • GCAM uses relative-cost and absolute-cost logits, nested from sector to technology
  • Example exponents: −3 between electricity fuels, −10 among coal technologies

Check yourself

1. Costs 60, 70, 80; γ = −4; equal weights. Coal's share is about…

7.72 / 14.32 = 0.539.

2. Making the exponent more negative (−4 to −10) makes shares…

Steeper exponents amplify cost differences.

3. Share weights are…

They absorb unmeasured preferences and phase new options in.

4. In the absolute-cost logit, shares depend on…

exp(β c) makes share ratios depend on c_i − c_j.

5. Which order matches GCAM's electricity nest?

Sector, then fuel subsector, then technology, then cooling system.

Words and links

New words

Logit
A formula that turns costs (or profits) into market shares.
Logit exponent
The parameter that sets how strongly shares respond to cost differences.
Share weight
A calibrated multiplier on each option's attractiveness, set to match base-year data.
Nest
One level of a choice tree, with its own logit.
Calibration
Setting parameters so the model reproduces observed base-year data.
