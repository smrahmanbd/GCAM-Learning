Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Every climate pathway you see in an IPCC report, from a fast transition to a world that keeps burning coal, came out of a computer model. These models are called integrated assessment models. Integrated means they join the economy, energy, land, water and the climate into one calculation, so a change in one part shows up in all the others. Assessment means their job is to inform decisions. An integrated assessment model is not a crystal ball. It does not predict the year 2100. It is a what-if machine. You choose the inputs: how many people there will be, how rich they are, how cheap solar panels become, and which policies exist. The model returns a future that adds up: energy used equals energy produced, emissions match the fuels burned, and warming matches the emissions. That consistency is what it adds. There are two main families. Cost-benefit models weigh the cost of cutting emissions against the damage of warming. Process-based models, such as GCAM, describe energy and land in detail and ask how a goal could be met. In this module you will open the machine from the outside.

A what-if machine, not a crystal ball

An integrated assessment model (IAM) joins the pieces of the climate problem into one calculation: the economy, energy, land and water, the emissions they produce, and the climate that responds. Integrated means a change in one part shows up in the others. Assessment means its purpose is to inform decisions.

Think of a recipe calculator. You type in the ingredients you choose: how many people, how rich, how cheap solar is, which policies exist. The calculator returns a cake that adds up. Where the analogy breaks: a real cake can be tasted, but a 2100 world cannot, so you judge a model by its consistency and its assumptions, not by its result.

A useful mental model

An IAM is a consistency machine. It never tells you what will happen. It tells you what follows if your assumptions are true.

Why consistency is the whole point

Every box in the diagram must agree with every other box. Energy used must equal energy made. Emissions must match the fuel burned. Warming must match the emissions. That single demand, consistency, is what an IAM adds that a spreadsheet of separate guesses does not.

CO2 = energy used × CO2 per unit of energy

With illustrative numbers: 600 units of energy at 0.06 Gt CO2 each gives 36 Gt. Halve the CO2 per unit and emissions fall to 18 Gt, a 50% cut. A consistent model cannot claim that cut and keep the old emissions.

Inputs and outputs

Inputs are assumptions you choose: population in 2050, income per person, the cost of solar panels, the price of carbon. Outputs are what the model works out from them: the energy mix, energy prices, emissions and warming in 2100.

A useful test: could a different modeller reasonably choose a different value? If yes, it is an input. If the model calculates it, it is an output.

Common trap

"The model predicts 2.7 °C by 2100." No: it projects what follows from its assumptions. Change the assumptions and the number changes. An IAM does not decide policy; policies are inputs the user chooses.

Two families of models

Cost-benefit models, such as DICE, are small: a few equations for one world. They ask how much should we cut? by weighing the cost of cutting emissions against the damage of warming.

Process-based models, such as GCAM, MESSAGEix, REMIND and IMAGE, have thousands of inputs and many regions. They ask how could a goal be met? They describe power plants, cars, crops and forests, and choose among them by cost. This course uses GCAM, a process-based model.

Remember

  • An IAM is a consistent what-if machine, not a forecast.
  • Inputs are assumptions you choose, including policies; outputs are projections.
  • Consistency (energy, emissions and warming all agree) is what an IAM adds.
  • Cost-benefit models ask how much to cut; process-based models like GCAM ask how a goal can be met.

Check yourself

1. An integrated assessment model is best described as:

IAMs work out what follows from assumptions and keep every part consistent. They do not forecast or choose policy.

2. Which of these is an input to an IAM?

Policies such as a carbon price are chosen by the user. The other three are calculated by the model.

3. What does 'integrated' mean in 'integrated assessment model'?

Integrated means the economy, energy, land, water and climate are linked in one calculation.

4. GCAM belongs to which family of models?

GCAM describes technologies, crops and markets in detail, region by region: a process-based IAM.

5. A model shows 2.7 °C of warming by 2100. The right way to say this is:

IAM results are conditional projections. They depend on the assumptions that went in.

Words and links

New words

Integrated assessment model (IAM)
A computer model that joins the economy, energy, land, water, emissions and climate into one consistent calculation to inform decisions.
Projection
What follows from a set of assumptions. Unlike a prediction, it does not claim to say what will happen.
Input (assumption)
A value the modeller chooses, such as population, technology costs or a policy.
Consistency
Every part of the model agrees: energy used equals energy made, and emissions match the fuels burned.
Cost-benefit model
A small IAM that weighs the cost of cutting emissions against the damage of warming, for example DICE.
Process-based model
A detailed IAM that represents technologies, sectors and regions and chooses among them by cost, for example GCAM.
