Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Only three ideas from economics are needed to read an integrated assessment model. First, a market price keeps moving until what is offered equals what is wanted, like a farmers market at closing time. That balancing price is called the equilibrium. GCAM finds such a price for every fuel and crop, in every region, in each time step. Second, a carbon price adds a charge to every option in proportion to its emissions. Coal costs sixty dollars a megawatt hour and emits zero point nine tonnes of carbon dioxide; with a fifty dollar carbon price it costs one hundred and five. Gas rises to ninety. Solar stays at eighty, because it emits nothing. The winner changes. Real models rarely give everything to the cheapest option. They share the market, so cheaper options get bigger shares. Third, a dollar today is worth more than a dollar later. Discounting turns future amounts into today's money. At three percent a year, one hundred dollars of damage in a hundred years is worth only about five dollars today; at one and a half percent, about twenty two dollars. The rate a modeller chooses changes how much the future counts.

Prices balance markets

Think of a farmers' market at closing time. If tomatoes pile up unsold, sellers cut the price; if they sell out by noon, they raise it next week. The price keeps moving until what is offered equals what is wanted: the equilibrium.

Toy numbers from the course lab: demand = 1000 − 4p and supply = 6p. Setting them equal, 1000 = 10p, so p = 100 and quantity = 600. GCAM finds a balancing price like this for every fuel and crop, in every region, in every time step.

A carbon price changes who wins

new cost = start cost + emission factor × carbon price

Coal600.960 + 0.9 × 50 = 105

Gas beats coal above $20 per tonne; solar beats gas above $25 per tonne (illustrative costs).

Common trap

"A carbon price makes everything more expensive." Only options that emit. Solar's cost does not change. And "a high discount rate means we care more about the future" is backwards: a high rate makes the future count less.

Try it now

At a carbon price of $22 per tonne, which technology is cheapest? (Answer: coal 79.8, gas 78.8, solar 80, so gas.)

Real models share the market

In the toy table the cheapest option takes everything. Real people and firms do not all choose the same thing: costs differ by place, and tastes differ. So GCAM shares each market among options by their relative cost: the cheapest gets the biggest share, but not all of it. You will meet the rule, called a logit, in Go 2.

Discounting weighs the future

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar later: you could invest it or use it now. Discounting turns future money into today's value:

value today = future amount ÷ (1 + r)years

$100 of climate damage in 100 years is worth $22.56 today at 1.5%, $5.20 at 3% and $0.76 at 5%. One number chosen by the modeller changes the value of the future roughly thirtyfold. Cost-benefit models such as DICE use discounting to weigh cutting now against damage later; process models use prices to decide which technologies supply each market.

A useful mental model

A carbon price is a toll charged in proportion to the smoke each option makes. Everything else in the model reacts to the new tolls.

Remember

  • Prices move until supply equals demand: the equilibrium.
  • A carbon price adds emission factor × price to each option's cost, so clean options gain.
  • Toy break-evens: gas beats coal above $20/t, solar beats gas above $25/t.
  • Real IAMs share markets by relative cost instead of picking one winner.
  • Discounting divides future values by (1 + r)^years; the chosen rate changes how much the future counts.

Check yourself

1. Demand is 1000 − 4p and supply is 6p. The equilibrium price is:

1000 − 4p = 6p, so 10p = 1000 and p = 100.

2. Coal costs 60 $/MWh and emits 0.9 t CO2/MWh. With a $50/t carbon price it costs:

60 + 0.9 × 50 = 105.

3. Why is solar's cost unchanged by a carbon price?

The charge is emission factor × price, and 0 × anything is 0.

4. At a 3% discount rate, $100 of damage in 100 years is worth about:

100 ÷ 1.03^100 ≈ 100 ÷ 19.2 ≈ 5.20.

5. How does GCAM decide which power technologies supply electricity?

GCAM shares markets by relative cost with a logit rule; cheaper options get bigger, but not complete, shares.

Words and links

New words

Equilibrium
The price at which the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer.
Carbon price
A charge per tonne of CO2 emitted, which raises the cost of each option in proportion to its emissions.
Emission factor
Tonnes of CO2 released per unit of output, for example 0.9 t CO2 per MWh from a coal plant.
Discount rate
The yearly rate used to convert future money into today's value; a higher rate makes the future count less.
Present value
What a future amount is worth today: future amount ÷ (1 + r)^years.
Market share
The fraction of a market supplied by one option, for example solar's share of new electricity.
