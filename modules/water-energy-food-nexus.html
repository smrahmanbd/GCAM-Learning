Land, water and food: the nexus

Farms, power plants and cities share the same land and the same river basins. Pull on one and the others feel it. This is why GCAM models water, land and energy together.

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Water, energy and food are tied together like three overlapping circles. Farms need water for irrigation and energy for pumps, machines and fertiliser. Power plants need water to cool them, and some electricity is used to move and clean water. Land grows food, but it can also grow energy crops, or stay as forest that stores carbon. Because of these links, a decision in one system shows up in the others. Grow more bioenergy to cut emissions, and you may need more land and water, which can raise food prices. Build more thermal power plants in a dry region, and the river basin may run short. GCAM represents these links directly. It divides the world into 235 river basins, each with its own water supply, and into hundreds of land areas. When demand for water gets close to what a basin can renewably supply, a water price rises and more expensive sources come in, first groundwater and then desalination. Land is shared out by profit, so a carbon price that rewards bioenergy or forests changes what is grown. Thinking in terms of the nexus helps you spot the side effects of a policy before they surprise you.

Three overlapping circles

Water for food: irrigation. Water for energy: cooling thermal plants, growing biomass, producing fuels. Energy for water: pumping, treatment, desalination. Land for energy: bioenergy crops. Every overlap is a place where one system can constrain another.

Bioenergy: the strongest link

Bioenergy can cut emissions, especially combined with carbon capture (BECCS). But it needs land, and sometimes irrigation. More energy crops can mean less forest or higher food prices. Many deep-mitigation scenarios rely on large amounts of bioenergy, so this trade-off matters.

Common trap

"Bioenergy is carbon-neutral, so more is always better." Its land, water and food effects can be large, and land-use change can release carbon.

Water has a price in GCAM

GCAM tracks water withdrawals and consumption in 235 basins. Supply comes in three tiers: renewable surface water and groundwater first, then non-renewable groundwater, then desalination. As demand nears the cheaper tiers' limits, the basin's water price rises and steers use. Module 2.7 works through the numbers.

In GCAM

GCAM's land model uses more than 300 land units and its water model 235 basins, so the nexus is solved in the same equilibrium as the energy system.

Side effects to look for

  • A carbon price that rewards bioenergy can raise land and food prices.
  • More thermal power in a dry basin can create water stress; dry cooling uses much less water but costs more.
  • Electrifying transport shifts demand to power plants, and so to cooling water.

Words and links

New words

Nexus
The set of links between water, energy and food (and land) that make them depend on each other.
Withdrawal
Water taken from a source; some returns, some is consumed.
BECCS
Bioenergy with carbon capture and storage, which can remove CO2 from the air.
Basin
The area drained by one river system; GCAM's unit for water supply and demand.
