Cutting emissions: options and costs

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

There are many ways to cut emissions: using energy more efficiently, fixing methane leaks, building wind and solar power, switching to electric cars, changing industrial fuels, capturing carbon, or even pulling it out of the air. They do not all cost the same. A marginal abatement cost curve lines them up from cheapest to most expensive. Each bar is one option. Its width is how many tonnes it can cut each year, and its height is the cost of cutting one tonne. Some bars sit below zero, because those options save money over their lifetime. Now add a carbon price. Every option whose cost is below the price is worth doing, and every option above it is not, yet. So raising the price walks along the curve, buying more and more cuts. Notice two things. First, the last tonne bought is the most expensive one, so the average cost is far below the price. Second, technology changes the curve: when solar gets cheaper, its bar drops and more is bought at the same price. Models like GCAM do not use a fixed curve, but the same logic of cheapest first, up to the price, runs through them.

Many ways to cut a tonne

Efficiency, methane fixes, renewables, electric vehicles, fuel switching in industry, carbon capture and storage (CCS), and direct air capture all cut CO2, at very different costs. Some, such as efficient lighting, save money over their life: their cost per tonne is negative.

Reading the curve

On a marginal abatement cost (MAC) curve each option is a bar: width = tonnes cut per year, height = cost per tonne. Bars are sorted from cheapest to dearest, so the curve rises to the right.

cuts bought at price P = total width of all bars with cost ≤ P

Marginal and average cost

At a price of $100 per tonne you buy every option up to $100. The marginal cost (the last tonne) is $100, but the average cost may be $30, because many cheap or negative-cost options came first. Total cost = the area of the bars bought, not price × tonnes.

Common trap

"A $100 carbon price costs $100 for every tonne cut." No: only the last tonne costs $100. Most of the tonnes bought were cheaper.

Technology moves the curve

Learning and research lower costs, which drops bars down the curve: since 2010 solar and battery costs fell so much that options once near the top moved toward the bottom. Scenarios that assume faster cost declines therefore reach the same cut with a lower carbon price.

In GCAM

GCAM builds no MAC curve for CO2; it lets technologies compete by logit at each carbon price. But for non-CO2 gases (methane, nitrous oxide, F-gases) GCAM does use marginal abatement cost curves.

Remember

  • Options differ hugely in cost per tonne; some save money.
  • A carbon price buys every option cheaper than itself.
  • The price is the marginal cost; the average cost is lower.
  • Falling technology costs shift bars down and cheapen deep cuts.

Check yourself

1. On a MAC curve, the width of a bar shows…

Width = abatement potential; height = cost per tonne.

2. With a carbon price of $60 per tonne, which options are worth doing?

Every option below the price saves more in carbon costs than it costs.

3. Why is the average cost of the cuts bought below the carbon price?

The price is the cost of the last tonne; the earlier ones were cheaper.

4. In GCAM, MAC curves are used mainly for…

CO2 abatement emerges from technology competition; non-CO2 gases use MAC curves.

Words and links

New words

Marginal abatement cost (MAC)
The cost of cutting one more tonne of emissions.
MAC curve
Options sorted by cost per tonne, showing how much can be cut at each price.
Abatement
A reduction in emissions below what they would otherwise have been.
Direct air capture
Machines that remove CO2 from the ambient air for storage; currently expensive.
