Read the transcript

Every integrated assessment model is built from two simple ideas. The first is the difference between a stock and a flow. A stock is an amount that has built up, like water in a bathtub, carbon dioxide in the air, or the cars on the road. A flow is a rate that changes the stock, like the tap and the drain, the emissions each year, or the new cars sold. A stock rises whenever the inflow is bigger than the outflow, even if the inflow is falling. That is why carbon dioxide in the air keeps rising while emissions are being cut: the tap is still running faster than oceans and forests can drain it. The second idea is the feedback loop. In a reinforcing loop, a change feeds itself: warming weakens natural sinks, so more carbon stays in the air, which causes more warming. In a balancing loop, a change is pushed back: higher prices reduce demand, which lowers prices again. Real systems also have delays. Power plants and cars last for decades, so today's choices shape emissions for a long time. Keep these ideas in mind and the behaviour of any model becomes much easier to read.

Stocks and flows

A stock is an amount that has accumulated: CO2 in the air, cars on the road, power plants standing, forests growing. A flow is a rate that changes a stock: emissions per year, cars sold per year, plants built or retired per year.

stock next year = stock this year + inflow − outflow

Test: if you could photograph it at one instant, it is a stock. If you need a stopwatch to measure it, it is a flow.

Why CO2 keeps rising while emissions fall

Suppose emissions fall from 40 to 30 Gt a year while sinks absorb about 20. The inflow is still bigger than the outflow, so the stock still rises, just more slowly. The level only starts to fall once inflow drops below outflow. This is the bathtub logic of the carbon budget from module 1.2, written as a model.

Common trap

"Emissions peaked, so the problem is shrinking." A falling inflow is not a falling stock. Ask: is the inflow below the outflow yet?

Two kinds of loop

A reinforcing loop amplifies a change: warming → weaker sinks → more CO2 stays → more warming. Cheaper solar → more solar built → learning → cheaper solar is another (see learning curves in Go 2). A balancing loop pushes back: higher prices → lower demand → lower prices. GCAM's markets are a web of balancing loops; the solver finds the prices where they all settle.

In GCAM

Hector, GCAM's climate model, is built from carbon stocks (atmosphere, ocean, land) linked by flows. Module 2.8 opens it and traces five feedback loops through the whole model.

Delays and inertia

Stocks change slowly. A coal plant runs for 40 years, a car for 15, a building for a century. GCAM tracks vintages: capital built in one period keeps operating in later ones. So a policy that starts late must work harder, because the old stock still emits. Delays also mean warming keeps responding for years after CO2 stops rising.

Remember

  • CO2 keeps rising until emissions fall below what sinks absorb.
  • Reinforcing loops amplify change; balancing loops damp it.
  • Long-lived capital and climate delays make early action matter.

1. Which of these is a stock?

2. Emissions fall from 40 to 30 Gt a year while sinks take 20. What happens to atmospheric CO2?

Inflow (30) is still above outflow (20), so the stock still rises.

3. 'Warming weakens sinks, so more CO2 stays, so more warming' is a…

The change feeds itself, so it is reinforcing.

4. Why do vintages matter for policy timing?

Plants and vehicles built today keep running, so late policies must work harder.

Words and links

New words

Stock
An amount that has accumulated and can be measured at one instant, such as CO2 in the air.
Flow
A rate that adds to or drains a stock, such as emissions per year.
Reinforcing loop
A feedback in which a change causes more of the same change.
Balancing loop
A feedback in which a change triggers a response that pushes it back.
Vintage
Capital built in a given period that keeps operating, with its own efficiency, in later periods.
