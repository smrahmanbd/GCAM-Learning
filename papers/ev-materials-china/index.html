Paper study 2 · Transport · GCAM v5.2 · China

If battery metals get expensive, do fewer people buy electric cars?

A hands-on companion to Wang, Feng, Wang, Yang, Sun, Yang, Chen, Zhang & Li (2023). The authors add the prices of four battery metals to GCAM and ask what happens to China's electric vehicle (EV) uptake and road CO₂ if those prices surge. The explorer on this page runs a small teaching model with made-up numbers, tuned to behave like the paper.

Paper: Nature Communications 14, 1246 (2023), open access. Last study: cars across 1.5–3 °C futures asked how clean each car is. This one asks how many EVs get chosen, which is GCAM's job.

1The paper in one minute

  1. EV batteries need lithium, cobalt, nickel and manganese, and the prices of these metals could rise sharply as demand grows.
  2. The authors fed rising metal prices into GCAM, where dearer batteries make EVs dearer, so the model's buyers choose fewer of them.
  3. In their worst case, EVs were 35% of China's vehicles in 2030 (not 49%) and 51% in 2060 (not 67%), and road CO₂ from 2020 to 2060 rose by 28%.

3Words you need

GCAMGlobal Change Analysis Model. A model of the world's energy, land, water and economy that finds prices where supply equals demand, every 5 years.
EV / BEVElectric vehicle. Here it means a battery electric car, bus or truck that runs only on electricity.
ICEVInternal combustion engine vehicle: a normal petrol or diesel car.
FCEVFuel cell electric vehicle. It makes electricity on board from hydrogen.
Critical materialsMetals that are important for clean technology but whose supply is at risk. Here: lithium (Li), cobalt (Co), nickel (Ni), manganese (Mn).
Battery chemistryThe recipe of the battery's positive side (cathode). NCM622 = nickel, cobalt, manganese in a 6:2:2 ratio. LFP = lithium iron phosphate, with no cobalt or nickel.
Battery pack costThe price of the whole battery, usually in $ per kWh of storage. Metals are one part of it.
Passenger-kilometre (pkm)One person carried one kilometre. GCAM measures passenger transport in pkm, and costs in $ per pkm.
Levelised costAll costs of a vehicle (buying, fuel, upkeep) spread over every pkm it delivers.
Logit choiceA formula that gives a bigger market share to cheaper options, but never gives everything to the cheapest one.
Logit exponent (λ)How strongly buyers react to cost differences. Big λ: small cost gaps cause big share shifts.
Share weightA number that captures non-cost preferences (range, charging, habit). It is calibrated so the model matches history.
Baseline (BLS)The paper's reference case: no metal price surge, and China on track for carbon neutrality by 2060.
Carbon neutralityNet zero CO₂: any remaining emissions are balanced by removals.
Cumulative emissionsAll emissions added up over a period (here 2020–2060). This is what matters for warming.
Recycling (RE)Getting metals back from old batteries, so less newly mined metal is needed.
ScenarioA consistent "what if" story about the future. Not a forecast.
Exogenous / endogenousExogenous = given to the model as an input (here, metal prices). Endogenous = worked out by the model (here, EV shares).

4The question, and why it matters

Question: if prices of lithium, cobalt, nickel and manganese surge, how many fewer EVs will China have, and how much more CO₂ will its roads emit on the way to the 2060 net-zero goal?

Why it matters: most long-term studies assume batteries simply keep getting cheaper. In 2021–2022, lithium prices jumped several times over. China is the world's biggest EV market, so if dearer metals slow EV sales there, global climate plans feel it too.

Link to what you know

In the last study, the car mix was given and the question was the footprint of each car. Here the footprint per kilometre comes from the energy system, and the question is the mix: GCAM decides how much travel each vehicle type gets from their costs. That decision is a logit choice.

What is new in this paper

Standard GCAM already lets buyers pick vehicles by cost with a logit. The authors' addition is the first box: they link battery cost to the prices of four metals and to the battery chemistry, and they run price paths through to 2060. Everything after that is normal GCAM.

How the toy model works (for the curious)

Every 5 years from 2020 to 2060:

  1. Each metal's price index rises from 1 to its scenario multiplier (lithium by 2035, the others by 2060). The High multipliers follow the paper's 2060 rises: cobalt ×5.7, manganese ×4.1, nickel ×2.6, lithium ×4.8. Low and Medium are made up.
  2. Battery cost per pkm = falling base cost × (non-metal part + Σ metal share × price index). NCM622 uses all four metals. LFP uses only lithium.
  3. Recycling reduces the price surge felt by the battery, slowly at first and strongly by 2060.
  4. Share of tech i = wi·ci−λ / Σ wj·cj−λ.
  5. CO₂ = travel × Σ share × emission factor. The EV and FCEV factors fall as power and hydrogen get cleaner.

7What they found

Fewer EVs

High surge: EVs were 35% of vehicles in 2030 and 51% in 2060. The baseline had 49% and 67%. Medium and Low surges gave 41%/60% and 43%/66%.

EVs only a bit dearer…

In 2030 a High surge made electric cars about 7–10% dearer per pkm. By 2060 the gap grew to 15–21% for most car sizes. Petrol car costs barely changed.

…but much more CO₂

Road CO₂ added up over 2020–2060 was about 23, 21 and 19 Gt for High, Medium and Low: 28%, 12% and 5% above the baseline.

Cars hit hardest

Passenger cars make up over 60% of road CO₂ and gave most of the extra. Buses and trucks lean toward fuel-cell vehicles, so they were hit less.

Chemistry matters

Cobalt-free LFP and LMO batteries kept more EVs on the road. Cumulative CO₂ came out about 3–14% lower than with NCM622.

Recycling helps

With recycling in the High case, the 2060 EV share rose from 51% to 59%, and cumulative CO₂ fell from about 23 to 22 Gt.

The authors' message, in a phrase: the price surge "could severely undermine the development of EVs". Their fixes are recycling, cobalt-free chemistries and safer, more varied metal supply.

8Predict, then check

Prediction 1

In the High scenario, a petrol car's cost barely moves and an electric car gets about 8% dearer in 2030. What happens to the EV share in 2030?

The paper: 49% → 35%, a fall of 14 points. A logit with a large exponent turns a small cost gap into a big share shift. Try λ = 2 and λ = 12 in the explorer to see this.

Prediction 2

Which chemistry keeps more EVs on the road when cobalt and nickel get very expensive?

LFP. It has no cobalt or nickel, so only the lithium surge reaches it. In the paper, cobalt-free LFP and LMO gave 3–14% less cumulative CO₂ than NCM622. Switch the explorer to LFP with the High scenario to check.

9Worked mini-exercise: logit by hand

Two cars, one surge

A petrol car costs $0.100 per pkm. An EV costs $0.090. Both have share weight 1, and λ = 6. Then a metal surge makes the EV 10% dearer ($0.099). What is the EV's new share, in %?

Hint

With equal weights, the EV share is 1 / (1 + (cEV/cICEV)λ). Equivalently r / (1 + r) with r = (cICEV/cEV)6.

Step-by-step solution
StepBefore surgeAfter surge
Cost ratio ICEV / EV0.100 / 0.090 = 1.1110.100 / 0.099 = 1.010
Raise to the power 6 (r)1.111⁶ = 1.881.010⁶ = 1.06
EV share = r / (1 + r)1.88 / 2.88 = 65.3%1.06 / 2.06 = 51.5%

A 10% cost rise cut the EV share by about 14 points. The EV went from clearly cheapest to almost tied, and the logit splits a near-tie close to 50:50. This is the same mechanism as in the paper.

10Quiz

11Limitations and open questions

  • Prices are assumed, not solved. Metal price paths are given to the model. In reality, fewer EVs would mean lower metal demand and maybe lower prices. That feedback is not in the model.
  • Results lean on the logit. The exponent and share weights decide how strongly buyers react. Different values would give different share drops.
  • Fuel cells fill the gap. In the model, many trucks and buses go to hydrogen. If fuel cells turn out dearer than assumed, the CO₂ picture could change.
  • Supply risk is about more than price. Most cobalt comes from one country (about 71% from the DR Congo, per the paper). Shortages or trade limits could bite harder than a smooth price rise.
  • Open question: new chemistries (for example sodium-ion) and faster recycling could cut metal needs. How fast would they need to grow to cancel the surge?
