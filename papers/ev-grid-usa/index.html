When the US switches to electric cars, what happens to the rest of the energy system?

A hands-on companion to Sadavarte, Shindell & Loughlin (2025). The authors push far more battery electric cars into GCAM-USA and follow the ripples: more power plants run, fewer refineries run, fuel prices shift. Then they add up CO₂ and air pollution for the whole country. The explorer on this page runs a small teaching model with made-up numbers.

Paper: PLOS Climate 4(10), e0000714 (2025), open access. Builds on: study 1 (how clean is each car?) and study 2 (how GCAM's logit picks cars). This one asks what a big EV push does to everything else.

1The paper in one minute

  1. Most car footprint studies hold the power grid fixed, but millions of new electric cars change the grid, the refineries and fuel prices too.
  2. The authors used GCAM-USA to compare a reference future with three futures where battery electric cars reach 46%, 61% or 75% of new car sales by 2050.
  3. In the most ambitious case, car CO₂ fell by about half and power-sector CO₂ rose only about 6%, so US CO₂ still fell, and car pollution like NOx fell by about half too.

2Learning goals

After this page you can…

3Words you need

GCAM-USAA version of GCAM that splits the United States into its 50 states plus DC, while the rest of the world stays in 31 regions. It steps forward 5 years at a time.
GLIMPSEA tool from the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) that packages GCAM-USA with ready-made scenarios. The paper used version 1.1.
BEVBattery electric vehicle. Runs only on electricity from a big battery.
ICE / ICEVInternal combustion engine vehicle: a normal petrol (gasoline) car.
LDVLight-duty vehicle: cars, SUVs and pick-up trucks. In the paper, "Cars" and "Large Cars and Trucks".
Sales shareThe share of new cars sold in a year. Different from the stock share, the share of all cars on the road, which changes slowly.
Logit choiceThe formula GCAM uses to split travel between options. Cheaper options get bigger shares, but never all of it.
Share weightA number that adds non-cost preferences (habit, range worries) to the logit. 1 means no bias.
Market share constraintA rule that forces a technology's share to be at least some value. The paper uses it to make sure BEV targets are met.
Well-to-tankEmissions from making and delivering the fuel: drilling, refining, or generating electricity.
Tank-to-wheelEmissions from using the fuel in the car. For a petrol car, the exhaust. For a BEV, zero.
Life cycleAll stages added up: making the car and battery, the fuel, and driving.
Systemic responseKnock-on changes elsewhere in the energy system: the power plant mix, refinery output, fuel prices, other sectors switching fuel.
Consequential viewAsking "what changes in the whole system because of this choice?" instead of "what is the footprint of one item on today's grid?".
Grid mixThe shares of gas, coal, wind, solar, nuclear and hydro in electricity. It sets how much CO₂ each kWh carries.
NOx, SO₂, COAir pollutants. Nitrogen oxides (smog), sulphur dioxide (mainly from coal) and carbon monoxide (from engines).
kWh / GWh / EJEnergy units. A car battery holds tens of kWh. 1 GWh = 1 million kWh. An EJ (exajoule) is a huge unit used for national energy.
Social cost (damages)A money value for the harm from one tonne of a pollutant: health effects, climate damage and so on.
RGGI / RPSExisting US power policies in the reference case: a CO₂ cap in north-eastern states (RGGI) and state renewable targets (RPS).

4The question, and why it matters

Question: if the US sells far more battery electric cars up to 2050, how do national CO₂ and air pollution change once we count the power sector, the refineries, battery making and all the ripple effects?

Why it matters: people often ask "are EVs really cleaner, given the grid?". A footprint for one car on today's grid cannot answer what happens when tens of millions of cars switch. Extra demand can keep old coal plants open for longer, and lower oil demand can make petrol cheaper for everyone else. Policy makers need the whole picture.

Link to what you know

Study 1 used a fixed set of grid futures to rate each car. Study 2 let GCAM choose how many EVs to buy. Here the EV numbers are pushed up on purpose, and GCAM works out how the rest of the energy system responds. That is why GCAM-USA, with its detailed state power sectors, is the tool.

What is new in this paper

The car choice and the power sector are normal GCAM-USA. The authors' contribution is the bookkeeping: they force higher BEV sales, take the difference from the reference run in every sector, and turn it into a life-cycle footprint per car and per kWh of battery. Car exhaust factors come from EPA's MOVES model, and battery making uses 75 kg CO₂e per kWh.

How the toy model works (for the curious)
  1. BEV sales shares follow the paper's targets (REF about 31%, OPT 46%, PRG 61%, AMB 75% in 2050; in-between years are rough).
  2. The BEV stock share is the average of the last three 5-year sales shares, because cars last about 15 years.
  3. Car travel grows 1% a year. Petrol use = travel × (1 − stock share) × a slowly improving fuel economy. BEVs use about 30% as much energy per km.
  4. Exhaust CO₂ = petrol × 70. Fuel production CO₂ = a fixed part (refining for other users) + 15% of exhaust CO₂.
  5. Electricity CO₂ = grid intensity × (other demand + EV demand). If the grid responds, the extra EV demand carries a different intensity than the average: dirtier early (old coal stays open longer), cleaner later (new wind, solar and gas get built). If it is fixed, the extra demand gets the average intensity.

7What they found

Far more batteries

Battery capacity in new cars reached about 770 GWh a year in 2050 in REF, and about 1,930 GWh in the most ambitious case (AMB). Each new BEV's battery was assumed to grow from 40 kWh (2020) to 150 kWh (2050).

A bit more electricity

In 2050, AMB used about 9% more electricity than REF (27.9 vs 25.7 EJ). The reference grid in 2050 was about 44% gas, 22% wind, 17% solar and 6% coal.

Shifts between sectors

AMB versus REF in 2050: car CO₂ down about 54%, fuel production CO₂ down about 19%, electricity CO₂ up only about 6%.

National CO₂ falls, modestly

US CO₂ was about 4,690 Tg in 2020. In 2050 it was about 4,200 Tg in REF and 4,010 Tg in AMB (OPT 4,120, PRG 4,070). Cars are only one slice of all emissions.

Cleaner air from cars

In AMB, car NOx, SO₂ and CO fell by about half by 2050. Power-sector NOx rose about 2%. SO₂ rose a little around 2030, because extra demand kept some coal plants open longer, but that bump was gone by 2050.

Per car and per battery

A new BEV starts with about 30% more CO₂ than a petrol car (battery making), but its total drops below the petrol car's after about two years. In 2030, each extra kWh of battery avoided about 220 kg of CO₂. Petrol cars caused about 2 to 3.5 times the damage cost of BEVs over their lives.

The big picture: once the whole system is counted, power-sector increases are small next to the drop in exhaust and refinery emissions. The gap widens as the grid gets cleaner.

8Predict, then check

Prediction 1

In 2050, AMB has 75% BEV sales and REF has 31%. AMB uses about 9% more electricity. How much more CO₂ does the power sector emit in AMB?

About 6% more. Electricity demand rises only about 9%, and by 2050 much of the extra comes from new wind, solar and gas, which are cleaner than the average plant. In the explorer, set AMB, 2050 and "grid responds" to see a similar pattern.

Prediction 2

Car CO₂ falls by about half in AMB versus REF in 2050. How much lower is total US CO₂?

About 5% lower: 4,010 vs 4,200 Tg. Cars are only part of US emissions. Industry, buildings, freight and power plants for other uses all stay, and some ripple effects (like cheaper oil) eat into the gain. A big fall in one sector looks small in the national total.

9Worked mini-exercise: carbon breakeven time

When does the BEV catch up?

Use these illustrative numbers. A BEV has a 60 kWh battery, and making batteries emits 75 kg CO₂e per kWh (the paper's factor). Apart from the battery, making the two cars emits the same. Each year, the petrol car emits 5.0 t CO₂ (exhaust plus fuel production). The BEV's electricity emits 2.0 t CO₂ a year. After how many years has the BEV caught up?

Hint

First find the BEV's extra "head start" CO₂ from the battery. Then find how much less CO₂ it emits each year. Breakeven = head start ÷ yearly saving.

Step-by-step solution
StepWorkingResult
Battery head start60 kWh × 75 kg/kWh4,500 kg = 4.5 t
Yearly saving5.0 t − 2.0 t3.0 t per year
Breakeven4.5 t ÷ 3.0 t per year1.5 years

The paper found about two years with its own numbers. Now ask: what if the grid were dirtier? With BEV electricity at 3.5 t a year, the saving is 1.5 t, and breakeven takes 3 years. A cleaner grid makes the BEV catch up sooner, which is why the paper's damage gap grows over time.

11Limitations and open questions

  • Not every stage is counted. End-of-life and recycling, charging stations, road and refinery building, and fuel transport are left out. Methane leaks from gas supply are left out too.
  • Targets are imposed. The BEV shares are forced with constraints, not chosen by GCAM's buyers. The study tells us "what if" these shares happen, not "will" they.
  • The reference future is dated. The reference case leaves out the 2021 infrastructure law and the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act. Plug-in hybrids are not modelled on their own.
  • Only some harms are valued. Damage costs cover CO₂, CO, SO₂ and NOx only. Mining impacts and water use are not in the totals.
  • Funding. The work was funded by a lithium producer, declared by the authors as a competing interest. That does not make the results wrong, but it is worth knowing when you read them.
  • Open question: how would results change with a faster-cleaning grid, smart charging at times of high solar, or a full battery recycling loop?

From: cars across 1.5–3 °C futures (study 1)

What is breakeven mileage?

Show answer
The lifetime distance at which a BEV's whole-life footprint drops below a hybrid's. Today's breakeven time is the same idea, measured in years.

From: battery metals and China's EVs (study 2)

Why can a 7–10% rise in EV cost cut the EV share by about 14 points in GCAM?

Show answer
The logit with a large exponent turns small cost gaps into big share shifts, especially when options are close in cost.

From: hydrogen in trucks, ships and factories (study 3)

What mattered more for how much hydrogen gets used: delivery options or end-use costs?

Show answer
End-use technology costs. Delivery options mainly changed where hydrogen was made (on site vs central plants).
