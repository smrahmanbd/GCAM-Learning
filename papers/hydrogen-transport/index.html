Paper study 3 · Transport fuels · GCAM v5.4 · World

Will hydrogen fuel trucks and ships, or mostly factories?

A hands-on companion to O’Rourke, Mignone, Kyle, Chapman, Fuhrman, Wolfram & McJeon (2023). The authors improve how GCAM handles hydrogen: how it is made, how it travels, and who uses it. Then they ask what really decides how much hydrogen gets used in a below-2 °C world. The explorer on this page runs a small teaching model with made-up numbers, tuned to behave roughly like the paper.

Paper: Environmental Science & Technology 57(48), 19508–19518 (2023), open access. Last study: battery metal prices and China’s EVs used a logit to share car travel between petrol, battery and fuel-cell cars. This one follows the fuel-cell branch: where does the hydrogen come from, and which vehicles pick it?

1The paper in one minute

  1. Hydrogen could replace oil in trucks, ships, buses and factories, but nobody knows how much of it a low-carbon world will use.
  2. The authors gave GCAM more hydrogen detail (making it, trucking or piping it, and using it) and tested what changes the amount used in a below-2 °C world.
  3. Hydrogen reached only 10–22 EJ in 2050 (2–4% of final energy), mostly made on site from clean electricity; cheaper end-use technology raised that amount far more than building pipelines did.

3Words you need

GCAMGlobal Change Analysis Model. It links energy, land, water, economy and climate for 32 world regions, in 5-year steps, and finds prices where supply meets demand.
Hydrogen (H₂)A gas that releases energy when burned or used in a fuel cell, leaving only water. It is an energy carrier: it must be made from something else first.
Fuel cellA device that turns hydrogen and air into electricity on board a vehicle. A fuel-cell truck is an electric truck that carries hydrogen instead of a big battery.
ElectrolysisSplitting water into hydrogen and oxygen with electricity. With clean power it is nearly zero-carbon.
Green hydrogenMade by electrolysis using wind, solar or clean grid power.
Blue hydrogenMade from natural gas, with most of the CO₂ captured and stored (CCS). In the paper, capture rises from 91% to 93%.
BECCS hydrogenMade from biomass with CO₂ capture. Plants took the carbon from the air, so storing it can make the hydrogen carbon-negative.
CCSCarbon capture and storage: catching CO₂ at a plant and pumping it underground.
Central vs on-site productionCentral = a big plant far from users, so the gas must be delivered. On-site = a small unit right where it is used (for example, at a truck refuelling station).
T&D (transmission and distribution)Moving hydrogen from plant to user. Here: by liquid-hydrogen truck or by gas pipeline.
BlendingMixing some hydrogen into the existing natural gas network (up to 20% by volume in one case, about 7% by energy).
End useWhere energy is finally used: a truck, a ship, a cement kiln, a home boiler.
Logit choiceGCAM’s rule for sharing a market among options: cheaper options get bigger shares, but never all of it.
Levelised costAll costs of an option (equipment, fuel, upkeep) spread over each unit of service, like $ per tonne-km.
Carbon priceA charge per tonne of CO₂ emitted. Here it starts in 2025 and rises 3% a year, set high enough to hit the climate goal.
EJ (exajoule)A unit of energy: 10¹⁸ joules. The world uses roughly 600 EJ of final energy a year in 2050 in these runs.
Final energyEnergy delivered to users (fuel in the tank, electricity at the socket), after conversion losses.
Service demandWhat users actually want: passenger-km, tonne-km, tonnes of cement. Fuels are only a means to it.
Below 2 °C / 1.5 °CClimate goals for warming by 2100. The paper’s main runs aim for 1.75 °C; a test run aims for 1.5 °C.
Sensitivity caseA run where one assumption is changed to see how much the result depends on it.

4The question, and why it matters

Question: in a world on track for below 2 °C, how much hydrogen gets used, by whom, and what drives that amount most: the supply side (how hydrogen is made and moved) or the demand side (how cheap hydrogen trucks, ships and furnaces become)?

Why it matters: governments are spending billions on hydrogen hubs and pipelines. If the bottleneck is really the cost of fuel-cell trucks or hydrogen furnaces, money spent only on pipelines may not buy much extra hydrogen use. For transport, it tells us where hydrogen may fit: heavy and hard-to-electrify jobs, not most cars.

Link to what you know

In the last study, the fuel-cell car was one of three options in a logit, and its cost was simply given. Here the cost of the hydrogen fuel itself comes from another logit one level up: production routes compete to supply the hydrogen. So you will see two logits stacked, which is how GCAM works everywhere.

What is new in this paper

Standard GCAM already had some hydrogen. The authors added detail: more production routes (including dedicated wind and solar electrolysis), costed delivery by truck and pipeline, on-site production, blending into gas grids, and more end uses (trucks, ships, rail, buses, cement and other industry). Then they switched delivery options on and off, and made end-use technology cheaper or not, to see which lever matters.

Run the paper’s test: supply lever vs demand lever

This runs six toy cases at the carbon price set above: three delivery options × two end-use cost cases (no cut, and a 30% cut). Compare how much each lever changes the total and the on-site share.

How the toy model works (for the curious)
  1. Production routes (made-up $/GJ): on-site green 25, on-site blue 25 (industry only), central green 20, central blue 20.5, central BECCS 30. Blue pays the carbon price on its leftover CO₂. BECCS earns money from it, because it removes CO₂.
  2. Delivery adds $10/GJ by truck or $5.5/GJ by pipeline to central routes. On-site routes need no delivery.
  3. Logit no. 1 shares supply among the available routes (exponent 12). The hydrogen price is the logit’s combined cost, which falls a little when more options are added.
  4. Logit no. 2: in each end use, cost of oil = 1 + carbon adder, electricity = fixed, hydrogen = equipment part × (1 − cost cut) + fuel part × H₂ price. Shares = w·c−6 / Σ w·c−6. Industry gets 40% of the cost cut.
  5. Share weights are set so the Reference run gives shares close to the paper’s 2050 table (for example, cars 1.5%, trucks 1.7%, buses 10%, freight rail 17%, industry 3%).
  6. H₂ use = share × the end use’s energy demand (cars 40, buses 6, trucks 45, rail 4, ships 16, industry 280 EJ).

7What they found

Not that much hydrogen

Below 2.5 EJ worldwide up to 2030, and 10–22 EJ by 2050 across the six main runs. That is only 2–4% of all final energy in 2050.

Factories before trucks

Industry used 40–76% of the hydrogen. In the lowest run, industry took 8.2 EJ and transport 2.6 EJ. Transport only overtook industry when hydrogen vehicles got cheaper.

Transport: a niche

Even in the most hydrogen-friendly main run, hydrogen met about 7% of road freight and 5% of international shipping service in 2050. Liquid fuels still met 66% and 94%. Buses (up to 25%) and freight rail (up to 20%) had the highest shares.

Demand side drives the amount

Cheaper end-use technology (the High Demand case) raised 2050 hydrogen use by 8–9 EJ, or 70–84%. Adding pipelines and trucks raised it by less than 2 EJ.

Supply side drives the mix

Delivery options mostly decided where hydrogen was made. Without them, nearly all was on site. With pipelines too, central plants supplied about half (47–57%).

Green and on site wins

Green hydrogen was the biggest source in 2050 (36–69%), and over 90% of it was made on site. Blue gave 24–29%, and BECCS 11–21% when delivery existed.

Stricter goal, more hydrogen

Aiming for 1.5 °C instead of below 2 °C more than doubled mid-century hydrogen use, to 30–48 EJ. Ships then relied on it much more heavily.

In one phrase: the scale of hydrogen use depends on the cost of the things that use it, while delivery infrastructure mainly reshapes the supply chain.

8Predict, then check

Prediction 1

Start from a world where hydrogen can only be made on site. Now allow trucks and pipelines from big central plants. What happens to total hydrogen use in 2050?

It rises only a little: under 2 EJ in the paper’s Reference case. What changes is the supply chain: central plants take a much larger slice. On-site electrolysis already avoids delivery costs, so pipelines add little that is cheaper. Try the delivery buttons in the explorer.

Prediction 2

In the paper’s lowest-hydrogen main run, which sector used more hydrogen in 2050?

Industry: 8.2 EJ against 2.6 EJ for transport. Industry has huge energy demand, so even a 3% hydrogen share is a lot of hydrogen. Transport hydrogen only caught up when vehicle costs fell.

Prediction 3

Switch the goal from below 2 °C to 1.5 °C. What happens to mid-century hydrogen use?

More than double: 30–48 EJ. A higher carbon price makes oil and gas much dearer, so hydrogen wins more of the jobs that are hard to electrify. Use the “1.5 °C-like” preset in the explorer to see the toy do this.

9Worked mini-exercise: a three-way logit for trucks

Diesel, battery or fuel cell?

With a carbon price included, three truck options cost (per tonne-km, made-up units): diesel 1.00, battery-electric 1.10, fuel-cell 1.20. All share weights are 1 and the logit exponent is 6. The fuel-cell share is 17.6%. Now fuel-cell trucks get 15% cheaper, so they cost 1.02. What is the new fuel-cell share, in %?

Hint

Work out c−6 for each option (that is 1 ÷ c⁶). The share of an option is its value divided by the sum of all three.

Step-by-step solution
OptionCost beforec−6Cost afterc−6
Diesel1.001.0001.001.000
Battery1.100.5641.100.564
Fuel cell1.200.3351.020.888
Sum1.8992.452

Fuel-cell share before: 0.335 ÷ 1.899 = 17.6%. After: 0.888 ÷ 2.452 = 36.2%.

A 15% cheaper truck doubled its share. It took share from both diesel (52.6% → 40.8%) and battery trucks (29.7% → 23.0%). This is the paper’s main point in miniature: making the end-use technology cheaper is a strong lever on how much hydrogen gets used.

11Limitations and open questions

  • No hydrogen trade between regions. Each of GCAM’s 32 regions makes its own hydrogen. Shipping hydrogen or ammonia across oceans is left for future work.
  • No hydrogen planes, no e-fuels. Aviation was left out as too uncertain, and so were fuels made from hydrogen (like ammonia or e-kerosene for ships and planes). Hydrogen use by power plants is also missing. Including these could raise transport demand.
  • Leaks not modelled. Hydrogen that escapes indirectly warms the climate. The authors discuss this but do not calculate it; truck delivery and transport use carry the most risk.
  • Cost assumptions drive everything. The logit shares depend on assumed vehicle, equipment and electrolyser costs. The main finding is itself about costs, so different cost paths give different amounts.
  • National policies left out. Subsidies like the US Inflation Reduction Act could make hydrogen cheaper in some places sooner.
  • Open question: if batteries keep getting cheaper (the last study’s topic), will fuel-cell trucks ever get their chance, or will hydrogen stay mostly in factories and ships?
