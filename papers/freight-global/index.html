Paper study 6 · Transport · GCAM · Global · freight

When the world prices carbon, why is freight so hard to clean up?

A hands-on companion to Muratori et al. (2017). The authors use GCAM to look only at freight: trucks, trains and ships moving goods. They ask how fast freight will grow as the world gets richer, and how much a carbon price can cut its CO₂ compared with other sectors. The explorer on this page runs a small teaching model with made-up numbers.

Paper: Environmental Science & Technology 51(6), 3526–3533 (2017), open access. Builds on: study 3 (hydrogen trucks and ships) and study 5 (every mode under 1.5 °C). This one is older and simpler: it zooms in on freight and asks which lever does the cutting.

1The paper in one minute

  1. Freight has always grown in step with the economy, and there is little sign yet that this link is breaking, so GCAM projects freight energy use to grow 4 to 7 times by 2100 if nothing is done.
  2. The authors ran GCAM with no climate policy and with four climate targets, each with high and low freight growth, and split the CO₂ cuts into three levers: less freight, cleaner modes and technologies, and cleaner fuel.
  3. Under the strictest target, freight CO₂ over the century fell about 70%, but less than in other sectors; most of the cut came from cleaner liquid fuel (biofuels with carbon capture), then from less freight, while switching to trains or ships did little.

2Learning goals

After this page you can…

  • …explain how GCAM turns GDP growth into freight demand, and what decoupling means.
  • …name the three levers a carbon price can pull in freight (less freight, mode or technology shift, cleaner fuel) and say which one did most in the paper.
  • …split a CO₂ cut into those levers yourself with a simple decomposition.
  • …explain why freight is cut less than buildings or industry under the same carbon price.

3Words you need

GCAMThe Global Change Analysis Model. It links energy, land, water and climate for 32 world regions in 5-year steps, from 2010 to 2100.
FreightMoving goods, not people. Trucks, freight trains, river and coastal ships, international ships and cargo planes.
Passenger transportMoving people: cars, buses, trains, planes. The other half of GCAM's transport sector.
Tonne-kmThe unit of freight service. One tonne of goods moved one kilometre = 1 tonne-km.
ModeThe way goods travel: truck, rail, ship or plane. Ships and rail use far less energy per tonne-km than trucks.
TechnologyThe vehicle type inside a mode, for example a diesel truck or a natural gas truck.
Logit choiceGCAM's rule for sharing demand among options. Cheaper options get bigger shares, but not all of it. A low "exponent" means people stick with what they have.
GDPGross domestic product: the size of an economy. GCAM uses it, with population, to drive demand.
Income elasticityHow much freight grows when GDP grows. 1 means 10% more GDP gives 10% more tonne-km.
DecouplingWhen freight grows more slowly than GDP (income elasticity drops below 1). Think of a richer economy selling more services and data, not more heavy goods.
Price elasticityHow much freight falls when moving goods gets dearer. A carbon price cuts freight this way.
Energy intensityEnergy used per tonne-km. Better engines and fuller trucks lower it. In this paper it improves on its own over time, not because of the carbon price.
Carbon intensity of fuelCO₂ per unit of fuel energy. Diesel is high, biofuel is near zero, and biofuel with carbon capture can be below zero.
Carbon priceA cost per tonne of CO₂ emitted. GCAM raises it until a climate target is met.
Climate target (LCP)The paper's four mitigation cases: LCP 6.0, 4.5, 3.7 and 2.6. Like the better-known RCPs, the number is the extra heat-trapping (W/m²) allowed in 2100. 2.6 is the strictest.
No MitigationThe reference case with no climate policy, used as the yardstick.
BiofuelLiquid fuel made from plants. It can go in the same tanks as diesel.
CCS / BECCSCarbon capture and storage traps CO₂ at a plant. Making biofuel with CCS (bioenergy with CCS) can take CO₂ out of the air overall.
Direct emissionsCO₂ from the vehicle's exhaust.
Indirect emissionsCO₂ from making the fuel, for example at a refinery or biofuel plant.
DecompositionSplitting a change in CO₂ into parts, one per cause, so the parts add up to the total.
Cumulative emissionsAll the CO₂ added up over many years, here 2010–2100. What matters for warming.
Mitigation costThe economic cost of meeting a climate target. "Discounted" means future costs count a bit less than today's.
EJExajoule, a huge energy unit. The world uses roughly 600 EJ of energy a year.

4The question, and why it matters

Question: how much will freight grow this century, how much can climate policy cut its CO₂, and which changes do the cutting: less freight, a shift to trains and ships, new truck technologies, or cleaner fuel?

Why it matters: freight uses about 40% of transport energy today and makes a similar share of transport CO₂. Trucks use most of it; international shipping uses about a quarter. Many earlier studies focused on cars. If freight keeps growing with the economy and is hard to clean up, it could become a larger and larger slice of the CO₂ left over in a climate-safe world.

Link to what you know

Study 3 showed that hydrogen trucks only win if their costs fall. Study 5 showed that, in a 1.5 °C world, freight trucks gave the biggest CO₂ savings in tonnes. This 2017 paper is the earlier, simpler view: it uses a carbon price alone, no special truck rules, and asks how far that gets freight.

What is special in this paper

The GCAM machinery is standard: demand from GDP, logit choices for modes and technologies, and one carbon price for the whole economy. What the authors add is a careful look at freight demand (two futures: freight keeps tracking GDP, or it decouples faster) and a decomposition of the CO₂ cut. Energy use per tonne-km improves over time in both futures, but it is set in advance, so the carbon price cannot speed it up. Sensitivity runs try easier mode shifting and more efficient trucks.

All growth rates, costs, elasticities and carbon prices here are made up for teaching. They are tuned so the pattern looks like the paper's (fuel lever largest, mode shift small, low demand about 40% lower CO₂ in 2100). It is not GCAM data, and CO₂ is shown as an index (2010 = 100).

How the toy model works (for the curious)
  1. Demand. GDP grows 2.8% a year. Freight tonne-km grows at GDP growth × income elasticity. High demand: elasticity 0.95 all century. Low demand: it falls from 0.95 to 0.55 by 2100 (decoupling).
  2. Modes. Truck, rail and ship share tonne-km by a logit on cost per tonne-km (exponent 1.5, or 3 with easy mode shift). Rail uses 30% and ships 18% of a truck's energy per tonne-km. Energy per tonne-km also falls 0.6% a year for all modes, whatever the price.
  3. Fuel. Oil, biofuel and biofuel with CCS share liquid fuel by a logit (exponent 4). CO₂ per unit: oil 1, biofuel 0, biofuel with CCS −0.7. Biomass gets dearer as the carbon price rises, so biofuel cannot take over for free.
  4. Demand response. Tonne-km falls with the average cost of freight, price elasticity 0.8.
  5. Carbon price in 2100: LCP 6.0 = $40, 4.5 = $120, 3.7 = $250, 2.6 = $600 per t CO₂, rising from zero in 2020.
  6. Decomposition (adds up exactly): demand lever = lost tonne-km × old intensity × old fuel CO₂; mode lever = new tonne-km × drop in intensity × old fuel CO₂; fuel lever = new tonne-km × new intensity × drop in fuel CO₂.
  7. Buildings (toy). Gas and clean electricity share heating by a logit (exponent 6, electricity only a little dearer than gas). The cut is the fall in gas use at the same carbon price. "Exhaust CO₂ from oil" for freight leaves out the below-zero credit from biofuel with CCS, so the two numbers are fair to compare.

7What they found

Freight follows GDP

In the past, freight energy and CO₂ grew together with GDP. The authors found little sign that the world as a whole is breaking this link yet, though some decoupling later this century is plausible.

Big growth without policy

With no climate policy, freight energy use grew by a factor of 4 to 7 by 2100, depending on the demand future. Freight CO₂ grew faster than passenger transport or any other big end-use sector.

Demand matters a lot

With no policy, direct freight CO₂ in 2100 was about 40% lower in the low-demand future. World fossil CO₂ in 2100 was 16% higher in the high-demand future, mainly because freight used about 70% more fuel.

Strict target: about 70% less

Under LCP 2.6, freight CO₂ over the century fell 68% (low demand) to 73% (high demand) versus no policy. Freight energy in 2100 was about one-third lower.

Cleaner fuel does most

Most of the freight cut came from lowering the CO₂ in liquid fuel, above all biofuels made with carbon capture. Less freight, because it got dearer, came second.

Mode shift barely helps

Switching to rail or ships, or to other truck technologies, did little in GCAM. Even a sensitivity run with easier mode shifting changed the results only a little.

Freight lags other sectors

Buildings and industry cut CO₂ faster, mainly because they can switch to electricity. So the stricter the target, the larger freight's share of the CO₂ that is left.

Slow freight growth saves money

The low-demand future cut the discounted cost of meeting the targets by 49% (LCP 6.0), 24% (4.5), 17% (3.7) and 20% (2.6).

Near term: still growing

Even under LCP 2.6, freight energy in 2040 was 70–100% above 2010, compared with 86–128% with no policy. Strict climate policy slows freight growth, but does not stop it soon.

The big picture: in a carbon-price-only world, GCAM keeps trucks running on liquid fuel and cleans the fuel instead of the truck. That leans heavily on biofuels with carbon capture, a technology that does not yet exist at scale. Later studies (like study 5) add electric and hydrogen trucks and sales rules, which change this picture.

8Predict, then check

Prediction 1

Under the strictest target (LCP 2.6), which lever cut freight CO₂ the most in GCAM?

Cleaner liquid fuel. Trucks kept burning liquid fuel, but more of it came from biomass, much of it made with carbon capture. Less freight came second, and mode shift was small. In the explorer, untick "Biofuel with CCS" at LCP 2.6 and watch the fuel slice shrink and the cumulative cut drop.

Prediction 2

The same carbon price applies to every sector. Which is cut more in percent?

Buildings. Heating, cooling and appliances can switch to electricity from a clean grid. Freight has few cheap ways to leave liquid fuel, so the same price buys a smaller cut. Compare the last two numbers under the explorer's chart.

9Worked mini-exercise: split a CO₂ cut into levers

How much of the cut is due to cleaner fuel?

Use these illustrative numbers for one region in 2100. Freight CO₂ = tonne-km × energy per tonne-km × CO₂ per unit of fuel.

  • No policy: 100 units of tonne-km, energy intensity 1.0, fuel CO₂ factor 1.0 → 100 Mt CO₂.
  • With a carbon price: 80 units of tonne-km, energy intensity 0.95 (a little mode shift), fuel CO₂ factor 0.4 → 80 × 0.95 × 0.4 = 30.4 Mt.

The total cut is 69.6 Mt. Using the step order demand → mode → fuel, how many Mt of the cut come from cleaner fuel?

Hint

Change one thing at a time and keep the earlier changes. For the fuel step, tonne-km and intensity are already at their new values (80 and 0.95). Only the fuel factor drops, from 1.0 to 0.4.

Step-by-step solution
LeverWorkingCut (Mt)
1 · Less freight(100 − 80) × 1.0 × 1.020.0
2 · Mode shift80 × (1.0 − 0.95) × 1.04.0
3 · Cleaner fuel80 × 0.95 × (1.0 − 0.4)45.6
Check20.0 + 4.0 + 45.669.6 = 100 − 30.4 ✓

So cleaner fuel gives about two-thirds of the cut (45.6 ÷ 69.6 ≈ 66%), less freight about 29%, and mode shift about 6%. That is the same ranking the paper found. Note: if you change the order of the steps, the split moves a little; the total does not.

10Quiz

11Limitations and open questions

  • Demand is an assumption. The two freight futures are chosen by the authors. Faster decoupling (for example from 3D printing or less coal and oil to ship) would change everything downstream.
  • Efficiency is fixed in advance. Better engines and fuller trucks improve over time, but the carbon price cannot speed them up. Real policies like fuel economy standards could do more.
  • Mode shift is limited. Rail lines and ports constrain how much can move off trucks, so GCAM allows little shifting. Whether that is too strict is open.
  • Big bet on biofuels with CCS. The main lever depends on a technology not yet used at scale. Land-use CO₂ from growing bioenergy was not counted in the indirect emissions.
  • An older GCAM. This 2017 version had few zero-emission truck options. Newer GCAM versions add battery and hydrogen trucks, which later papers show can matter a lot.
  • Open question: what mix of fuel rules, truck standards and logistics changes would cut freight CO₂ without relying so much on bioenergy?

13Review: from earlier papers

Spaced repetition: try to answer before you open each one.

From: battery metals and China's EVs (study 2)

Why can a 7–10% rise in EV cost cut the EV share by about 14 points in GCAM?

Show answer
The logit with a large exponent turns small cost gaps into big share shifts, especially when options are close in cost. Today's paper shows the flip side: freight modes have a low exponent, so even big cost gaps move little freight to rail.

From: hydrogen in trucks, ships and factories (study 3)

What mattered more for how much hydrogen gets used: delivery options or end-use costs?

Show answer
End-use technology costs. Delivery options mainly changed where hydrogen was made (on site vs central plants). The 2017 freight paper had no cheap hydrogen trucks, which is one reason its trucks stayed on liquid fuel.

From: zero-emission transport worldwide (study 5)

How do you work out a break-even carbon price for a clean technology?

Show answer
Divide its extra cost per unit of service by the CO₂ it saves per unit. Example: $18 extra ÷ 0.06 t saved = $300 per t. In today's paper, biofuel with CCS has a low break-even price because it saves more than 100% of the CO₂.
