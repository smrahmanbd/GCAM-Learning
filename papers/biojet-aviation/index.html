Paper study 7 · Transport · GCAM · Global · aviation

Planes need liquid fuel. Can biojet fuel clean up flying?

A hands-on companion to Wise, Muratori & Kyle (2017). The authors add a separate jet fuel sector to GCAM, with jet fuel made from plants (biojet). They ask what a carbon price does to flying, with and without biojet, and with and without carbon capture at the biojet plant. The explorer on this page runs a small teaching model with made-up numbers.

Paper: Transportation Research Part D 52(A), 244–253 (2017), open access. Builds on: study 6 (freight, by the same team, same year) and study 5 (why long-haul flights ran on biofuel). Same tools as yesterday, one new mode: the plane.

1The paper in one minute

  1. Flying grows fast as people get richer: in GCAM, world demand for air travel grows four times between 2010 and 2050, and planes have no easy option other than liquid fuel.
  2. With only fossil jet fuel, a carbon price strong enough to halve energy CO₂ by 2050 cuts aviation CO₂ by only about 10–20%, and only by making people fly less.
  3. Adding biojet fuel lowers the CO₂ per litre, uses only about a tenth of the world's bioenergy, and works far better when the biojet plant captures and stores its CO₂.

2Learning goals

After this page you can…

  • …explain why a carbon price on its own cuts flying CO₂ less than CO₂ elsewhere, and where that small cut comes from.
  • …describe how GCAM chooses between fossil jet, biojet and biojet with CCS using costs and a logit.
  • …work out the net CO₂ of a fuel mix when biojet with CCS gives "negative" emissions.
  • …say why biojet did not crowd out bioenergy in the rest of the energy system in this study.

3Words you need

GCAMThe Global Change Analysis Model. It links energy, land, water and climate for world regions in 5-year steps. This paper used GCAM version 4.
AviationFlying people and goods by plane. In GCAM it is one mode inside transport, next to cars, buses, trains, trucks and ships.
Aviation service demandHow much flying people want, measured in passenger-km (and tonne-km for air cargo). One passenger flown one km = 1 passenger-km.
Jet fuelThe kerosene-type liquid fuel planes burn. Normally made from crude oil at a refinery.
BiojetJet fuel made from plants or plant waste instead of oil. It can go into the same planes and tanks ("drop-in" fuel).
FeedstockThe raw material a fuel is made from. For biojet: grasses, wood, crop residues, oil crops, sugar or corn.
Lignocellulosic biomassThe tough, woody part of plants: wood, straw, energy grasses. Not food, but it needs land.
FT (Fischer–Tropsch)A process that turns biomass into a gas and then into liquid fuel. In this paper about 95% of biojet came this way.
ETJ and HEFATwo other biojet routes. ETJ (ethanol-to-jet) turns alcohol into jet fuel. HEFA treats plant oils and fats with hydrogen. Both were small here.
Conversion efficiencyHow much fuel you get from the feedstock. Here about 2 GJ of biomass gives 1 GJ of biojet.
CCSCarbon capture and storage: catching CO₂ at a plant and pumping it deep underground.
BECCS / biojet with CCSBioenergy with CCS. Plants take CO₂ from the air; part of it is stored at the fuel plant. Overall, the fuel can remove more CO₂ than it releases.
Net-zero (carbon-neutral) bioenergyThe assumption that burning plant fuel only returns the CO₂ the plants took up while growing, so it adds nothing overall.
Carbon intensityCO₂ per unit of fuel energy. Fossil jet is high, biojet near zero, biojet with CCS below zero.
Carbon priceA cost per tonne of CO₂ emitted. GCAM raises it until a climate target is met, with the same price in every sector.
Logit choiceGCAM's rule for sharing a market among options by cost. The cheapest option gets the biggest share, but not all of it.
Price elasticityHow much demand falls when the price rises. If flying gets dearer, people fly a bit less.
Energy intensityFuel used per passenger-km. Newer planes and fuller flights lower it, so fuel grows slower than flying.
Reference scenarioThe "no climate policy" yardstick. No carbon price.
Mitigation scenarioHere: energy-system CO₂ in 2050 must be half of what it was in 2005.
Crowding outWhen one user of a limited resource (here, biomass) pushes other users out.
Land-use change emissionsCO₂ released when forests or grassland are cleared, for example to grow energy crops.
EJ, GJEnergy units. 1 GJ is about 26 litres of jet fuel. 1 EJ is a billion GJ.

4The question, and why it matters

Question: if the world puts a price on carbon, what happens to aviation? How much can its CO₂ fall with fossil jet fuel only, and how much more if biojet is available, made with or without carbon capture? And would biojet take bioenergy away from other sectors that need it?

Why it matters: aviation is a small part of world CO₂ today, but it grows fast and is almost entirely oil. Cars can use batteries. Planes, especially on long routes, cannot easily do so, because batteries are too heavy. That makes clean liquid fuel the main hope, and the main question is whether there is enough biomass for it.

Link to what you know

Yesterday's freight paper found that a carbon price kept trucks on liquid fuel and cleaned the fuel instead. Planes are an even stronger case: there is no rail line in the sky. Study 5 (2024) found long-haul flights ran mostly on biofuel even in its high-ambition case. This 2017 paper is where that idea was first tested in GCAM.

What is special in this paper

Standard GCAM 4 lumps jet fuel in with other refined liquids. The authors split out a separate jet fuel sector, so planes can buy biojet directly, through several biojet routes with their own costs and efficiencies, some with CCS. Everything else is standard GCAM: demand from income and population, a logit for fuel choice, one carbon price for the whole economy, and a land model where energy crops compete with food and forests.

How the toy model works (for the curious)
  1. Demand. With no policy, flying grows 4× from 2010 to 2050. Fuel per passenger-km falls 25% over the same time, so fuel grows 3×.
  2. Fuel costs ($ per GJ, made up): fossil jet 15 plus the carbon price × 0.072 t CO₂ per GJ. Biojet 32. Biojet with CCS 38, minus a credit of 0.072 t CO₂ per GJ × the carbon price (it stores about as much CO₂ as one GJ of fossil jet releases). Both biojets also get 0.03 × carbon price dearer, because biomass and land cost more when every sector wants them.
  3. Logit. Share of each fuel ∝ weight × (cost / 15)−5. Weights: fossil 1, biojet 1.5, biojet with CCS 1.2. Options not on the menu get weight 0.
  4. Demand response. Cost of flying = 70% non-fuel costs + 30% fuel. Flying = no-policy flying × (cost of flying ÷ cost with no policy)−elasticity.
  5. CO₂ per GJ, relative to fossil: fossil 1, biojet 0, biojet with CCS −1. Net CO₂ = fuel use × (fossil share − CCS share).
  6. Carbon price starts in 2020 and rises to your 2050 value. The "rest of economy" number is a toy curve, 1 − e−price/220, standing in for sectors that can switch to clean electricity.
  7. Biomass. 2010 jet fuel is taken as 13 EJ (rounded, for scale only). Biomass = 2 × biojet energy.

7What they found

Flying grows 4×

With no climate policy, world demand for flying grew by a factor of 4 from 2010 to 2050. Europe and the US fly most today, but most of the growth was in today's developing economies.

Fuel and CO₂ grow 3×

Planes got more efficient, so fossil jet fuel grew about 3× rather than 4×. With fossil fuel only, aviation CO₂ also grew about 3×.

Small, but fast-growing

Aviation stayed under 5% of total energy-sector CO₂ in all four scenarios. The concern is its growth, and that it is hard to clean.

Fossil only: a small cut

Under the Mitigation target (2050 CO₂ = half of 2005), aviation CO₂ with only fossil jet fell only about 10–20% below Reference by 2050. The rest of the energy system cut much more deeply.

…and it comes from flying less

With no clean fuel, the only way to cut was to slow the growth of flying. The CO₂ per litre stayed the same. That makes aviation costly to decarbonise.

Biojet helps even without policy

In the Reference world, having biojet on the menu cut aviation CO₂ by about 100 million tonnes in 2050.

Biojet + CCS: the big change

In the Mitigation world with biojet, by 2050 there was nearly half as much biojet with CCS as fossil jet. Each unit of it roughly cancelled one unit of fossil jet, so net aviation CO₂ fell to about half of today's level.

No CCS, much less gain

Without CCS at the biojet plant, net aviation CO₂ in 2050 would have been more than twice as high as with it.

Mostly woody biomass via FT

About 95% of biojet came from FT conversion of woody and grassy (lignocellulosic) biomass. It took about 2 GJ of biomass to make 1 GJ of biojet.

Little crowding out

Biojet used only about 10% of all bioenergy in the Mitigation world. Its availability lowered the carbon price only a little, and it did not squeeze other sectors' use of bioenergy much.

Land: a side cost

Growing more biomass raised land-use change CO₂, but the increase was small next to the aviation cut (roughly 10–20% of it). The study kept 90% of unmanaged forests and grasslands off-limits to farming.

Flying still costs more

Even with biojet, the carbon price still held back the growth of flying a little, because biojet costs more to make than fossil jet.

Note: the paper's results section says "nearly 20%" lower and its conclusion says "about 10%", so this page gives the range.

The big picture: a carbon price alone barely dents flying CO₂ unless a clean liquid fuel exists. Biojet is that fuel in GCAM, and carbon capture at the biojet plant turns it from "about zero" into "below zero", which is what makes the large cut possible. That leans on a technology (BECCS) that is not yet used at scale.

8Predict, then check

Prediction 1

Mitigation world, fossil jet only. By 2050, how much lower is aviation CO₂ than in the Reference world?

About 10–20%. With no clean fuel, the carbon price can only make flying dearer, so people fly a bit less. The rest of the energy system cut CO₂ far more. In the explorer, choose Mitigation + Fossil jet only and slide the elasticity: the cut moves, but stays modest.

Prediction 2

Mitigation world with biojet. What share of the world's bioenergy went into jet fuel?

About a tenth. Aviation is a small sector (under 5% of energy CO₂), so even a big biojet share needs only a modest slice of the world's biomass. That is why the authors found little crowding out of other sectors.

Prediction 3

Same Mitigation world with biojet, but the biojet plants do not capture CO₂. What happens to net aviation CO₂ in 2050?

More than twice as high. Biojet without CCS only replaces fossil CO₂ with "about zero". With CCS, each unit also cancels out about one unit of fossil jet. In the explorer, switch the fuel menu between "+ biojet" and "+ biojet with CCS" at the same carbon price.

9Worked mini-exercise: net CO₂ with negative-emission fuel

What is net aviation CO₂?

Use these illustrative numbers for the world in 2050.

  • Planes burn 30 EJ of jet fuel: 20 EJ fossil jet and 10 EJ biojet made with CCS.
  • Fossil jet releases about 72 Mt CO₂ per EJ (72 kg per GJ).
  • As in the paper, one unit of biojet with CCS roughly cancels one unit of fossil jet: its net CO₂ is about −72 Mt per EJ.

What is net aviation CO₂ in Mt?

Hint

Work out each fuel on its own (energy × CO₂ per EJ), then add. The biojet-with-CCS term is negative, so it subtracts.

Step-by-step solution
StepWorkingMt CO₂
1 · Fossil jet20 EJ × 72+1,440
2 · Biojet with CCS10 EJ × (−72)−720
3 · Net1,440 − 720720
Compare: no CCS20 × 72 + 10 × 01,440 (twice as much)

Why is biojet with CCS negative? The plants took CO₂ out of the air as they grew. Part of that carbon ends up in the fuel and goes back to the air when the plane burns it. The rest is released at the plant, where it is captured and stored underground. So, overall, CO₂ leaves the air. Extra: the biojet needed about 2 × 10 = 20 EJ of biomass.

11Limitations and open questions

  • Only CO₂. Planes also warm the climate through contrails and nitrogen oxides at high altitude. The paper does not include these, so the full climate effect of flying is larger.
  • Bioenergy is assumed carbon-neutral. Burning plant fuel is counted as adding no CO₂. Real supply chains (fertiliser, farming, transport) add some.
  • A big bet on CCS. Most of the gain comes from biojet with CCS, which is not yet used at scale. The authors say large-scale CCS remains questionable.
  • Costs are assumptions. Biojet routes have assumed costs and efficiencies. If they turn out higher, less biojet would be used.
  • No other clean options. The 2017 model had no hydrogen planes, battery planes for short routes, or e-fuels made from electricity. Study 5 (2024) adds these.
  • Land and food. Land-use CO₂ was small here, partly because most unmanaged land was protected. Other land rules could change that.
  • Open question: if many sectors (shipping, trucks, industry) all want biomass at once, is there still enough for planes?

13Review: from earlier papers

Spaced repetition: try to answer before you open each one.

From: freight and a carbon price (study 6)

Why is freight cut less than buildings under the same carbon price in GCAM?

Show answer
Buildings can switch to clean electricity easily; freight relies on liquid fuels and has few cheap low-carbon mode or technology options. Today's paper shows the same for planes, even more strongly.

From: zero-emission transport worldwide (study 5)

Why did long-haul flights run mostly on biofuel even in the high-ambition case?

Show answer
Batteries and hydrogen turbines were far too costly for long routes (break-even over $1,300 per t CO₂), so drop-in clean liquids won, and FT biofuel was much cheaper than e-fuel. Today's 2017 paper is the earlier test of that biojet idea, also mostly via FT.

From: EVs and the US grid (study 4)

Why did US CO₂ fall only about 5% when car CO₂ fell by about half?

Show answer
Cars are one slice of national emissions, and the power sector emitted about 6% more; other sectors stayed the same. Same lesson today in reverse: aviation is under 5% of energy CO₂, so even a large change in it barely moves the global total.
