Paper study 5 · Transport · GCAM 6.0 · Global · all modes

Can all of transport, even planes and ships, reach zero CO₂ by 2050?

A hands-on companion to Speizer et al. (2024). The authors take the whole transport sector in GCAM 6.0 (cars, buses, trucks, trains, ships and planes) and ask how fast it can stop emitting CO₂ inside a world that stays below 1.5 °C. They also add a new fuel to GCAM: e-fuels made from captured air CO₂ and hydrogen. The explorer on this page runs a small teaching model with made-up numbers.

Paper: Nature Communications 15, 4439 (2024), open access. Builds on: study 2 (the car logit), study 3 (hydrogen in trucks and ships) and study 4 (knock-on effects in other sectors). This one zooms out to every mode, worldwide.

1The paper in one minute

  1. Planes and ships are the hardest part of transport to clean up, and few global studies had modelled zero emissions for every mode together with the rest of the economy.
  2. The authors ran GCAM 6.0 with a 1.5 °C carbon limit and three levels of transport ambition (low, medium, high), which differ in how fast fossil fuels are banned and how fast electric and hydrogen vehicles arrive.
  3. In the high case, transport stopped emitting CO₂ by 2050: electricity did most of the work, hydrogen led for big ocean ships, and biofuels kept long flights going, while weaker transport ambition pushed extra cuts onto industry and power.

2Learning goals

After this page you can…

  • …name the low-carbon option that leads in each transport mode in GCAM, and say why long-haul flights are the odd one out.
  • …explain how a fixed carbon budget links transport to other sectors: what transport does not cut, someone else must.
  • …work out a break-even carbon price for a clean technology and compare it with the cost of direct air capture.
  • …describe how a carbon price can cut emissions by switching fuels or by cutting travel (demand destruction).

3Words you need

GCAM 6.0The version of the Global Change Analysis Model used here. 32 world regions, 5-year steps, energy, land, water and climate linked together.
ModeA way of travelling or moving goods: car, bus, rail, truck, ship, plane. GCAM splits planes into short-haul and long-haul, and ships into domestic and international.
ServiceHow much transport is done. Passenger-km (pass-km) for people, tonne-km (ton-km) for goods. One person carried 1 km = 1 pass-km.
TechnologyThe vehicle type inside a mode: oil-fuelled, battery electric (BEV), hydrogen fuel cell (FCEV), or, for planes, a hydrogen-burning turbine.
Logit choiceGCAM's way of sharing out demand among options. Cheaper options win bigger shares, but not all of it.
Share weightA logit setting that captures non-cost factors, like how ready a new technology is. Low weight = slow uptake.
DecarboniseCut CO₂ emissions towards zero.
Direct vs indirect emissionsDirect: from the vehicle's exhaust. Indirect: from making its fuel (power plants, refineries, hydrogen plants).
1.5 °C carbon constraintA cap on total CO₂ from fuels and industry, set so that warming stays under 1.5 °C in 2100. GCAM finds the carbon price that meets it.
Carbon priceA cost per tonne of CO₂ emitted. It makes fossil fuels dearer, so clean options win more logit share.
Break-even carbon priceThe carbon price at which a clean technology costs the same as the fossil one. Extra cost ÷ CO₂ saved.
Residual emissionsEmissions that remain after all the cuts. Under net zero, they must be balanced by CO₂ removal.
Biofuel (FT)Liquid fuel made from plants. Here only the Fischer–Tropsch (FT) route from cellulosic biomass (grasses, wood, crop waste) counts, not food crops.
E-fuelSynthetic petrol-like liquid made from CO₂ captured from the air plus hydrogen. Works in today's engines ("drop-in").
DACDirect air capture: machines that pull CO₂ out of the air. Used here to make e-fuels, and as a yardstick for the cost of removing CO₂.
BECCS / CCSCarbon capture and storage (CCS) traps CO₂ at a plant. With bioenergy (BECCS), the net result can be CO₂ removed from the air.
Blending mandateA rule that a minimum share of liquid fuel must be biofuel or e-fuel. Used for planes and ships here.
Phase-out dateThe year after which no new fossil-fuelled vehicles are sold in a mode.
SSP1"Sustainability" storyline of the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways: lower population growth and greener habits. Used for population and GDP here.
Income elasticityHow much travel grows when income grows. 1 means 10% more income gives 10% more travel. The paper lowers it to mimic people choosing to travel less.
Price elasticityHow much travel falls when travel gets dearer. A carbon price can shrink flying and shipping this way.
Demand destructionCutting emissions by doing less travel or freight, because it costs more, instead of switching fuel.
Load factorPeople per vehicle. Ridesharing raises it, so fewer car-km are needed for the same pass-km.
Stranded assetA ship, plant or vehicle that must retire early because the rules or prices changed.
EJ / Gt CO₂Exajoule: a huge energy unit (the world uses about 600 EJ a year). Gigatonne: a billion tonnes of CO₂.
AR6 databaseThe big collection of scenarios from many models gathered for the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report.

4The question, and why it matters

Question: in a world that holds warming below 1.5 °C, which low-carbon option should each transport mode use, how early can the whole sector reach zero CO₂, and what does that mean for fuels, power plants, hydrogen and other sectors?

Why it matters: transport makes over a fifth of the world's CO₂. Cars now have a clear path (batteries), but planes and ships need lots of energy in a small, light tank and last for decades. Industry groups have set net-zero goals for aviation and shipping. Planners need to know which technologies to back, and what happens if transport moves slowly: the CO₂ it keeps emitting has to be cut somewhere else.

Link to what you know

Study 2 showed how the car logit reacts to cost. Study 3 showed hydrogen competing for trucks and ships. Study 4 showed how one sector's change ripples into the power sector. This paper puts all three ideas together: every mode runs its own technology logit, and a single world carbon cap ties transport to industry, buildings and power.

What is new in this paper

Most of the machinery is standard GCAM 6.0. The authors add e-fuels (four ways to make them, using grid, wind or solar power for the air capture and the hydrogen), require plane and ship biofuels to come from the FT cellulosic route, cap world bioenergy at 100 EJ in 2100, and design three ambition levels with phase-out dates for each mode. Hydrogen and batteries for planes and ships are given cost and efficiency numbers in the paper's supplement.

How the toy model works (for the curious)
  1. Each technology has a cost per unit of service (oil = 100) and direct CO₂ per unit (oil 0.2 t, biofuel 0.02, e-fuel 0.01, battery and hydrogen 0). Its total cost = cost + CO₂ × carbon price.
  2. Shares follow a logit: share ∝ weight × (total cost)−6. Battery and hydrogen weights are 0.35 (low), 0.7 (medium) or 1 (high ambition), and their costs are 10% higher (low) or 8% lower (high) than in medium.
  3. Toy rules for 2050. High: no oil at all; cars and trucks use no liquids; ships and planes may burn only biofuel or e-fuel. Medium: oil is capped at half of the mode (cars, trucks) or half of the liquids (ships, planes). Low: no rules.
  4. Travel = no-policy travel × (average cost ÷ no-policy average cost)−elasticity, with elasticity 0.25 for road and 0.5 for ships and planes.
  5. Break-even price = (clean cost − oil cost) ÷ (oil CO₂ − clean CO₂).

7What they found

Zero by 2050 is possible

With no climate policy, transport's direct CO₂ grew over 40% from 2020 to 2100. In the high case it hit zero by 2050, and direct plus fuel-making emissions fell 99% from 2020.

Leftovers are costly

From 2050 to 2100, transport still emitted about 39 Gt CO₂ in the medium case and 157 Gt in the low case. Removing that with DAC would cost roughly $7–14 trillion (medium) or $27–54 trillion (low).

Big savings

Over the century, high saved 245 Gt CO₂ versus low, about 60% of a 1.5 °C budget. Freight trucks (78 Gt) and cars (70 Gt) saved most in tonnes; flights saved most in percent (75–77%). Planes and ships gave 34% of the savings.

Electricity leads most modes

By 2050 in the high case, most modes were mainly electric. Domestic ships were over 80% electric. Short flights were 56% electric, with hydrogen and clean liquids sharing the rest. Buses were about 44% hydrogen.

The exceptions

International shipping was the only mode where hydrogen beat batteries. Long-haul flights relied on clean liquids for over 88% of service. E-fuels stayed small (under 16% of clean liquids) because biofuels were much cheaper.

Break-even prices

In 2050, electric and hydrogen ships and short-haul planes broke even at about $135–496 per t CO₂, similar to DAC at $172–351. Electric domestic ships and hydrogen ocean ships needed under $250. Long-haul electric or hydrogen planes needed over $1,300.

Less travel if fuels can't switch

In the low case, flying and shipping fell by 40% or more over the century versus no policy, and the carbon price caused over half of that drop for ships and short flights. More ambition won back 2–7% of that travel.

The see-saw with industry

With low transport ambition, industry had to reach zero CO₂ by 2100. High versus low freed up 128 Gt CO₂ for industry and 74 Gt for power over the century, and needed less carbon capture.

Fuel ripples

In 2050, transport used 17 EJ of hydrogen in high (69% of all hydrogen), 9.4 EJ in medium and 5.5 EJ in low. Transport used 39 EJ of electricity in high versus 25 EJ in low, while industry used 20 EJ less. Plane and ship biofuel passed 13 EJ, about five times today's transport biofuel.

The big picture: under one world carbon cap, transport ambition does not change whether 1.5 °C is met in the model, it changes who does the cutting and which fuels are used. The authors sum it up as finding that "electrification contributes most to decarbonization across the sector". Results held up in seven sensitivity runs (other SSPs, more travel, tighter bioenergy): transport CO₂ changed by under 8 Gt, though the fuel needs changed a lot.

8Predict, then check

Prediction 1

In the high case in 2050, which mode gets more service from hydrogen than from batteries?

International shipping. Ocean ships sail thousands of km without a stop, so batteries are far too heavy and costly. Hydrogen fuel cells win, helped by clean liquids early on. Long-haul flights also can't use batteries, but hydrogen turbines were too costly there too, so they relied on biofuel. Try "Intl. ship" and "Long flight" in the explorer.

Prediction 2

E-fuels work in today's engines and need no farmland. What share of the clean liquid fuel for planes and ships did they supply in the main runs?

Under 16%. Making e-fuel needs lots of electricity for air capture and hydrogen, so it costs far more than FT biofuel. E-fuels grew only when biomass was tighter or in the fast-growth SSP5 world, where wind-powered e-fuels got cheapest. In the explorer, see how small the striped e-fuel band and dashed e-fuel line stay.

9Worked mini-exercise: a break-even carbon price

When does an electric short-haul plane pay off?

Use these illustrative numbers. Flying 1,000 passenger-km in an oil-fuelled short-haul plane costs $120 and emits 0.06 t CO₂. The same trip in a battery-electric plane costs $138 and has zero direct CO₂. At what carbon price (in $ per tonne) do they cost the same?

Hint

At the break-even price, the carbon cost added to the oil plane equals the extra cost of the electric plane. So divide the extra cost by the CO₂ saved.

Step-by-step solution
StepWorkingResult
Extra cost of the electric plane$138 − $120$18 per 1,000 pass-km
CO₂ saved0.06 t − 0 t0.06 t per 1,000 pass-km
Break-even price$18 ÷ 0.06 t$300 per t CO₂
Check$120 + 0.06 × $300$138, same as electric ✓

Compare with the paper: break-even prices for clean ships and short flights were $135–496 per t in 2050, and DAC cost $172–351. So $300 sits in the same range: paying for the electric plane is about as costly as keeping the oil plane and removing its CO₂ with DAC. For long-haul flights the gap is much bigger (over $1,300), because batteries and hydrogen are so costly on long routes.

11Limitations and open questions

  • An optimistic world. All main runs use SSP1 and assume people travel less, share rides more and use more public transport. With more travel demand, the same cuts need over 50% more hydrogen and electricity for transport by 2100.
  • Unproven fuels. FT cellulosic biofuel is not yet made at large scale, yet long flights depend on it. Hydrogen planes could end up doing more than the model shows.
  • Fuels left out. Ammonia for ships was not modelled, because its leaks could harm the nitrogen cycle. Methanol from captured CO₂ was also left out.
  • Simple cost yardsticks. Break-even prices count only direct emissions and use reference-run costs. DAC costs are assumptions, not market prices.
  • No bottlenecks. The model does not limit how fast chargers, hydrogen networks, battery minerals, factories or skilled workers can scale up. In the high case, clean passenger travel grows eightfold in ten years.
  • Choices held fixed. Demand cuts, load factors and efficiency are the same in every scenario, so their effect is not tested. Air quality, health and fairness are not valued.
  • Open question: which policies (carbon prices, mandates, subsidies) would actually deliver the high case, and at what cost to whom?

13Review: from earlier papers

Spaced repetition: try to answer before you open each one.

From: cars across 1.5–3 °C futures (study 1)

Why does a typical life cycle assessment overstate a BEV's footprint?

Show answer
It uses today's grid for all 10–20 years of driving, though the grid gets cleaner. Today's paper makes the same point at world scale: 99% of electricity is low-carbon by 2050 in all 1.5 °C runs.

From: hydrogen in trucks, ships and factories (study 3)

Why was most green hydrogen made on site in GCAM's results?

Show answer
On-site electrolysis skips the cost of trucking or piping hydrogen, which is large per unit; over 90% of green hydrogen was on site. Today's paper also found most extra hydrogen came from electrolysis using grid power.

From: US electric cars and the grid (study 4)

Why did US CO₂ fall only about 5% when car CO₂ fell by about half?

Show answer
Cars are one slice of national emissions, and the power sector emitted about 6% more; other sectors stayed the same. Today's paper flips this view: with a fixed world cap, what one sector does not cut, others must.
