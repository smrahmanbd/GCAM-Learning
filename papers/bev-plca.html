Which car has the smallest carbon footprint as the energy system changes?

A hands-on companion to Šimaitis, Lupton, Vagg, Butnar, Sacchi & Allen (2025). In this study a car's footprint is worked out year by year, so each year of driving uses that year's electricity, diesel or hydrogen, taken from an integrated assessment model's scenario. Every chart on this page is driven by a small teaching model whose numbers are made up but set close to the paper's headline results.

Paper: Communications Earth & Environment 6, 476 (2025), open access (CC BY 4.0). New to cars and LCA? Start with Part 1: the basics.

1The big idea in one minute

A car is built once (year 0) and then driven for 10–20 years. Most life cycle assessments (LCAs) use today's energy data for every stage, as if the whole life happened in 2025. But the electricity grid, diesel and hydrogen will all change while the car is being driven.

The authors fix this with a time-adjusted prospective LCA (pLCA). Production uses 2025 data, the use phase is split into 5-year periods, each with that period's energy mix, and end-of-life sits in the last year. The mixes come from four TIAM-UCL scenarios (3.0 °C down to 1.5 °C).

The finding

In about 5,000 comparisons, battery electric vehicles (BEVs) almost always had the lowest footprint. On average they were 32% (3.0 °C world) to 47% (1.5 °C world) below hybrids. That is because electricity is expected to decarbonise much faster than diesel or hydrogen.

4Animation: the breakeven race

A BEV starts with a carbon debt because its battery takes a lot of energy to make. Every kilometre it then pays back part of that debt, since charging emits less than burning diesel. The breakeven mileage is the total lifetime distance at which the BEV's whole-life footprint drops below the hybrid's.

Each dot is one region × scenario (Medium SUV, 15 years). Dirtier grids need more kilometres. Scenarios pull the dots to the left, because the grid cleans up while the car is still being driven.

5Animation: 5,000 cars (comparative Monte Carlo)

The authors drew 5,000 random situations (size, region, mileage, lifetime, scenario) and worked out every powertrain in the same situation. That is a dependent, or comparative, Monte Carlo, so each comparison is fair. Each dot is one situation. A dot above the diagonal means car A had the bigger footprint. Compare with the paper's Fig. 2.

6Explorer: which input matters most? (sensitivity)

The paper used Sobol indices, which say what share of the spread in a result is caused by each input. Here we estimate the simplest version, the first-order index: group the Monte Carlo runs by one input, take the average result in each group, and see how much those averages differ compared with the total spread.

9How this links to GCAM and the course

The paper uses TIAM-UCL, a cost-optimisation IAM (see module 3.9, the IAM family). GCAM is a different kind of IAM: it solves for market equilibrium using logit choices. The pLCA method does not care which model the scenario comes from. The premise tool now reads GCAM v8.2 scenarios too, alongside REMIND, IMAGE, MESSAGEix and TIAM-UCL.

A research idea for your Go 3 project

Repeat the BEV-vs-HEV comparison with GCAM scenarios in premise, and compare it with the TIAM-UCL result. Different IAMs decarbonise at different speeds (the paper stresses this), so does the breakeven mileage move? The implementation guide walks through doing this with Claude Code in VS Code.
