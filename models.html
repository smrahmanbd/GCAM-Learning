Joint Global Change Research Institute (PNNL), USA

GCAM

SolutionMarket equilibrium (partial)
ForesightRecursive dynamic (myopic)
Time steps5 years to 2100
Regions32 energy-economy regions; 235 water basins; 300+ land units
LandProfit-based nested logit
ClimateHector (inside the model)
TransportPassenger and freight by mode, size class, technology; value of time
CodeOpen source

How it solves (animation)

Discussion

GCAM asks: given these assumptions, where do all markets clear in each five-year period? It holds no expectations of the future, so a carbon price path must be given as an input or found by constraint for each period. Technologies share markets by logit, which spreads demand smoothly and avoids all-or-nothing switches.

Strengths: rich and linked energy, land and water detail; open code and data system (gcamdata); transparent add-on XML for scenarios; widely used for transport, water and land questions. Limits: GDP is an input rather than a result (except with the newer macro module), there is no intertemporal optimisation, and logit parameters and share weights need care.

For transport studies GCAM is a natural home: modes compete on generalised cost including time, and the transport module was the starting point for REMIND's EDGE-T.

How it solves (animation)

Discussion

MESSAGEix asks: what is the cheapest way to meet energy demand in every region and period, subject to constraints such as a carbon budget? It chooses everything at once with full knowledge of the future, so it invests early where that lowers total discounted cost.

The carbon price is the shadow price of the emissions constraint. Under a cumulative budget it rises at about the discount rate. Because a pure linear programme can switch abruptly to the cheapest option, modellers add growth and diffusion constraints to keep pathways realistic.

Strengths: engineering detail and investment timing, open framework, central to the SSPs and IPCC assessments. Limits: perfect foresight overstates how well society anticipates, and results can be sensitive to bounds and constraints.

How it solves (animation)

Discussion

REMIND asks: how should a social planner in each region split output between consumption and investment, including investment in energy, to maximise discounted welfare over the century? Energy enters the production function through nested constant-elasticity-of-substitution (CES) trees.

Because it optimises over time, the Ramsey rule (interest rate = time preference + elasticity × growth) shapes how early it invests. MAgPIE supplies land-use emissions and bioenergy supply curves through iteration, and EDGE-T computes transport mode and technology shares between REMIND iterations.

Strengths: consistent macro-energy-land picture, open code, strong in Paris-consistent pathways. Limits: perfect foresight, and a single planner per region hides distribution within regions.

How it solves (animation)

Discussion

IMAGE asks: what happens to land, water, biodiversity and climate if society develops this way? It simulates rather than optimises: the TIMER energy model and the land system evolve step by step, with land demand allocated to grid cells ranked by suitability.

Its strength is environmental depth: land cover, nutrients, water and biodiversity (with linked models such as GLOBIO) on a spatial grid. Economic feedbacks on agriculture come from the MAGNET model. Policy cases are usually driven by carbon price paths obtained from a simplified run.

Use it when spatial environmental impacts matter. Be aware that it is not a whole-economy model and has no intertemporal optimisation.

How it solves (animation)

Discussion

AIM-Hub asks: if a carbon price is imposed, how do all markets in the economy (goods, labour, capital, energy, land) adjust together? Every sector and household is represented, so the cost of a policy appears as lost GDP or consumption, and trade effects show up directly.

Energy and agriculture are more disaggregated than other sectors, and power generation has technology detail. The model is part of the wider AIM framework used for national and Asia-Pacific studies.

Use it for economy-wide costs, trade and distribution across sectors. It has less engineering detail than an energy-system model and no foresight.

RFF-CMCC European Institute on Economics and the Environment (EIEE), Italy

WITCH

SolutionOptimal growth with a bottom-up energy sector (hybrid)
ForesightPerfect (intertemporal)
GameRegions in a non-cooperative Nash game, or coalitions
Regions17 by default (configurable)
Time steps5 years, to 2150
InnovationEndogenous technical change (R&D, learning)
SolverGAMS / CONOPT
CodeOpen source (GitHub)

How it solves (animation)

Discussion

WITCH asks: what will regions do if each pursues its own welfare, knowing the others do the same? Each region is a forward-looking agent; the outcome is a Nash equilibrium, and coalitions can be formed to study cooperation and its stability.

Its distinctive features are the game-theoretic set-up and endogenous innovation: research and development spending and learning lower energy costs over time, so policy can steer technical change.

Use it for questions about cooperation, free-riding, climate clubs and innovation policy. It has less sectoral and land detail than process-rich models.

William Nordhaus (Yale) and Lint Barrage (ETH Zurich): DICE-2023

DICE

SolutionCost–benefit optimal growth
ForesightPerfect
RegionsOne global region (RICE is the regional version)
OutputOptimal emissions path, social cost of carbon
ClimateSimple carbon cycle and temperature module (revised in DICE-2023)
DamagesQuadratic damage function of warming
LatestDICE-2023, PNAS 2024
CodeOpenly available

How it solves (animation)

Discussion

DICE asks: how much should the world cut emissions, weighing the cost of cutting against the damage avoided? It solves an optimal growth problem in which output is lost both to abatement spending and to climate damages; the optimum sets the social cost of carbon.

DICE-2023 revised the carbon cycle, damages and discounting, lowering the cost-beneficial temperature path and raising the estimated social cost of carbon compared with DICE-2016. Results hinge on the discount rate and the damage function, which is why they are debated.

Use DICE to understand the cost–benefit logic and the drivers of the social cost of carbon; use process-based models such as GCAM for how a goal could be met in practice.

Quiz: who am I?

1. "I solve one big linear programme with perfect foresight." Who am I?

It is MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM.

2. "I clear markets every five years and never look ahead." Who am I?

It is GCAM.

3. "My regions play a game against each other." Who am I?

It is WITCH.

4. "I weigh abatement costs against climate damages for one global region." Who am I?

It is DICE.

5. "I allocate land to grid cells by suitability." Who am I?

It is IMAGE.

6. "Every market in the economy, labour included, clears each year in me." Who am I?

It is AIM-Hub.

7. "A planner maximises welfare in me, and my transport module began as a fork of GCAM's." Who am I?

It is REMIND-MAgPIE.
